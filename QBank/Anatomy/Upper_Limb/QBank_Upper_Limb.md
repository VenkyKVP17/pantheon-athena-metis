# Upper Limb QBank

## Q1. Clavicle Fracture Site
**Question ID:** Q3234477
**Question:** A cyclist thrown over the handlebars lands on the point of the shoulder and snaps the collar bone. Imaging shows the break lies in its middle segment, the usual weak point between the two opposing curves. This commonest site corresponds to which region of the bone?
- A) The sternal extremity
- B) The middle 1/3rd of the bone
- C) The acromial extremity
- D) The conoid tubercle region

**Answer:** B) The middle 1/3rd of the bone

**Explanation:**
The clavicle is the only long bone placed horizontally and the only one to ossify in membrane, carrying a double curve (convex forward medially and concave forward laterally). The transition between these two curves, at the middle 1/3rd of the bone, is its weakest point and the commonest fracture site (taking ~80% of clavicular fractures / Allman group I). 
After a midshaft break, the lateral fragment is pulled down by the weight of the limb while the medial fragment is held up by sternocleidomastoid, producing a step deformity. The middle third overlies the subclavian vessels and brachial plexus.

**Take Home Message:** The clavicle breaks most often at its middle third, where its two opposing curves meet and no muscle protects it.

## Q2. Breast Lymphatic Drainage
**Question ID:** Q8049978
**Question:** A surgeon staging a tumour in the upper outer part of the mammary gland asks which nodes it is likely to reach first. The greater part of the gland drains initially to which group?
- A) Anterior axillary (pectoral) group
- B) Posterior intercostal group
- C) Deltopectoral (infraclavicular) group
- D) Nodes of the opposite armpit

**Answer:** A) Anterior axillary (pectoral) group

**Explanation:**
About 75% of breast lymph drains to axillary nodes. The lateral (outer) quadrants in particular drain first to the anterior or pectoral group of axillary nodes, located just behind the lower border of pectoralis major. Medial quadrants drain chiefly to internal thoracic (parasternal) nodes.

**Take Home Message:** Most breast lymph, especially from the outer quadrants, drains first to the anterior (pectoral) axillary nodes; medial quadrants go to internal thoracic nodes.

## Q3. Mammary Gland Structure & Extent
**Question ID:** Q6275978
**Question:** A clinician revises the structure of the female mammary gland before an examination. Decide which of the statements below are correct.
1. The upper outer extension of the gland passes around the lower border of pectoralis major into the armpit.
2. The gland normally contains fifteen to twenty lobes, each opening by a separate duct at the summit of the teat.
3. Fibrous bands anchoring the gland to the overlying skin act as suspensory supports.
4. The base of the gland lies over the second to sixth ribs.

- A) 1, 2 and 4 only
- B) 1, 2, 3 and 4
- C) 2, 3 and 4 only

**Answer:** B) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Explanation:**
All four statements are accurate:
1. The axillary tail of Spence pierces the deep fascia at the lower border of pectoralis major (anterior axillary fold) into the axilla.
2. Parenchyma contains 15 to 20 lobes, each drained by its own lactiferous duct.
3. Fibrous septa (suspensory ligaments of Cooper) anchor the gland to the dermis.
4. The base extends vertically from 2nd to 6th rib, and horizontally from sternal edge to midaxillary line.

**Take Home Message:** Remember the breast tail crosses the anterior axillary fold, the gland has 15 to 20 lobes, and suspensory ligaments anchor it to the skin.

## Q4. Breast Carcinoma Signs (Skin Dimpling)
**Question ID:** Q9696980
**Question:** A woman with a lump in the mammary gland is asked to raise both arms above her head, and the examiner sees the skin over the lump pucker inward. Tension on which structures best explains this dimpling of the skin?
- A) Suspensory ligaments tethered by the tumour
- B) The pectoral fascia sliding over the ribs
- C) Contraction of the dartos-like muscle of the nipple
- D) Engorged subcutaneous veins

**Answer:** A) Suspensory ligaments tethered by the tumour

**Explanation:**
The suspensory ligaments (of Cooper) anchor the mammary gland to the overlying dermis. When a carcinoma invades and shortens these bands, it tethers the skin so that stretching the breast (raising arms) causes skin dimpling/puckering. Pull on lactiferous ducts causes nipple retraction.

**Take Home Message:** A breast carcinoma dimples the skin by tethering and shortening the suspensory ligaments, and retracts the nipple by pulling on the lactiferous ducts.

## Q5. Scapula Landmarks & Pectoral Girdle
**Question ID:** Q8100805
**Question:** The bony points around the pectoral girdle can be felt through the skin and are used as surgical landmarks. All of the following statements are correct EXCEPT:
- A) The spine of the scapula ends laterally at the coracoid process
- B) The coracoid process is felt in the hollow just below the outer end of the clavicle
- C) The acromion forms the bony summit of the shoulder

**Answer:** A) The spine of the scapula ends laterally at the coracoid process

**Explanation:**
The spine of the scapula runs laterally and expands into the **acromion**, NOT the coracoid process. The coracoid process is a separate beak-like projection on the anterior aspect of the scapula, palpable deep within the infraclavicular fossa (deltopectoral triangle).

**Take Home Message:** The coracoid process is felt in the infraclavicular fossa as the only scapular process palpable from the front, while the spine of the scapula ends laterally in the acromion.

## Q6. Clavipectoral Fascia Structures
**Question ID:** Q9363634
**Question:** A demonstrator traces a sheet of deep fascia that runs from the clavicle down to enclose subclavius and pectoralis minor. Decide which of the structures below pierce this fascia to make their superficial-to-deep connections.
1. The cephalic vein.
2. Branches of the thoracoacromial artery.
3. The lateral pectoral nerve.
4. The musculocutaneous nerve.

- A) 1 and 4 only
- B) 2, 3 and 4 only
- C) 1, 2 and 3 only

**Answer:** C) 1, 2 and 3 only

**Explanation:**
The clavipectoral fascia splits to enclose subclavius and pectoralis minor before continuing as the suspensory ligament of the axilla. It is pierced by 4 structures:
1. **Cephalic vein** (passing inward to join axillary vein)
2. **Thoracoacromial artery branches** (passing outward)
3. **Lateral pectoral nerve** (passing outward to supply pectoralis major)
4. **Lymphatics** from breast to apical nodes.
The musculocutaneous nerve pierces coracobrachialis, NOT the clavipectoral fascia.

**Take Home Message:** The clavipectoral fascia is pierced by the cephalic vein, thoracoacromial artery branches, lateral pectoral nerve and breast lymphatics, but NOT by the musculocutaneous nerve.

## Q7. Pectoralis Minor & Axillary Artery Divisions
**Question ID:** Q3231691
**Question:** During an armpit dissection a thin triangular muscle is seen running from the third to fifth ribs up to the coracoid process, crossing the continuation of the subclavian vessel and dividing it into proximal, posterior and distal portions. Which muscle is this?
- A) Latissimus dorsi
- B) Subclavius
- C) Pectoralis minor
- D) Coracobrachialis

**Answer:** C) Pectoralis minor

**Explanation:**
Pectoralis minor arises from 3rd, 4th, and 5th ribs and inserts on the coracoid process. As it crosses the axillary artery, it divides it into 3 parts:
- **1st part** (proximal) -> 1 branch (Superior thoracic)
- **2nd part** (deep) -> 2 branches (Thoracoacromial, Lateral thoracic)
- **3rd part** (distal) -> 3 branches (Subscapular, Anterior circumflex humeral, Posterior circumflex humeral).

**Take Home Message:** Pectoralis minor crosses and divides the axillary artery into three parts, with 1, 2, and 3 branches arising from the parts in order.

## Q8. Axillary Artery Branches vs Subclavian Branches
**Question ID:** Q5196120
**Question:** The continuation of the subclavian beyond the first rib gives a series of named branches along its course. All of the following arise from this vessel EXCEPT:
- A) Superior thoracic artery
- B) Posterior circumflex humeral artery
- C) Subscapular artery
- D) Suprascapular artery

**Answer:** D) Suprascapular artery

**Explanation:**
The **suprascapular artery** is a branch of the **thyrocervical trunk** (from the 1st part of subclavian artery), NOT a branch of the axillary artery. The 6 branches of the axillary artery are: Superior thoracic, Thoracoacromial, Lateral thoracic, Subscapular, Anterior circumflex humeral, and Posterior circumflex humeral.

**Take Home Message:** The axillary artery branches are superior thoracic, thoracoacromial, lateral thoracic, subscapular, and the two circumflex humerals; suprascapular artery is a subclavian branch.

## Q9. Long Thoracic Nerve Injury & Winging of Scapula
**Question ID:** Q2842018
**Question:** A boxer cannot push a heavy door, and when he presses both hands against a wall the inner edge of one shoulder blade lifts away from the chest. Which nerve has been damaged?
- A) Spinal accessory nerve
- B) Long thoracic nerve
- C) Dorsal scapular nerve
- D) Thoracodorsal nerve

**Answer:** B) Long thoracic nerve

**Explanation:**
**Serratus anterior** ("boxer's muscle") holds the medial border of the scapula flat against the chest wall and protracts it during pushing/punching. It is innervated by the **long thoracic nerve of Bell (C5, C6, C7)**. When damaged (e.g., during axillary surgery/trauma), the medial border and inferior angle spring away from the chest wall, causing classic **winging of the scapula** brought out by pushing against a wall.

**Take Home Message:** Serratus anterior paralysis from a long thoracic nerve injury causes winging of the scapula brought out by pushing against a wall, and blocks full overhead elevation.

## Q10. Dorsal Scapular Nerve
**Question ID:** Q7278082
**Question:** A nerve arising from the C5 root pierces the middle scalene and runs deep along the medial border of the scapula to supply the rhomboids and the muscle that elevates its upper angle. Which nerve is this?
- A) Dorsal scapular nerve
- B) Accessory nerve
- C) Long thoracic nerve
- D) Nerve to subclavius

**Answer:** A) Dorsal scapular nerve

**Explanation:**
The **dorsal scapular nerve (C5)** pierces scalenus medius and runs deep along the medial border of the scapula. It innervates **rhomboid major, rhomboid minor, and levator scapulae**, which retract and elevate the scapula.

**Take Home Message:** The dorsal scapular nerve (C5) supplies the rhomboids and levator scapulae, the retractors and elevator of the scapula.

## Q11. Supracondylar Humerus Fracture & AIN (OK Sign) Injury
**Question ID:** Q6317006
**Question:** A 7-year-old child presents following a fall on an outstretched hand (FOOSH) with a supracondylar fracture of the distal humerus. Physical examination demonstrates an inability to make the "OK" sign (inability to flex the interphalangeal joint of the thumb and the distal interphalangeal joint of the index finger). Which nerve is most likely injured in this patient?
- A) Anterior interosseous nerve (deep branch of median nerve)
- B) Posterior interosseous nerve (deep branch of radial nerve)
- C) Ulnar nerve (deep branch)
- D) Musculocutaneous nerve

**Answer:** A) Anterior interosseous nerve (deep branch of median nerve)

**Explanation:**
The inability to make the **"OK" sign** is the classic clinical test for **Anterior Interosseous Nerve (AIN)** dysfunction. 

The OK sign requires:
- **Flexion of the thumb** at the interphalangeal (IP) joint -> by **Flexor Pollicis Longus (FPL)**.
- **Flexion of the index finger** at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint -> by lateral half of **Flexor Digitorum Profundus (FDP)**.

Both FPL and lateral half of FDP are innervated by the **Anterior Interosseous Nerve (AIN)**, which is the **deep motor branch of the median nerve** in the forearm.

**Supracondylar Fracture Clinical Correlation:**
- **Supracondylar fracture of the humerus** is the most common elbow fracture in pediatric patients, usually caused by falling on an outstretched hand (FOOSH injury).
- The median nerve (specifically its AIN branch) is the most commonly injured nerve in extension-type supracondylar fractures due to anterior displacement of the proximal humeral fracture segment.

**Take Home Message:** Inability to form an "OK" sign after a pediatric supracondylar humerus fracture indicates anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) injury, paralyzing flexor pollicis longus (FPL) and flexor digitorum profundus (FDP, index finger).

## Q12. Erb's Palsy (Upper Trunk Brachial Plexus Injury)
**Question ID:** Q4859201
**Question:** A newborn infant presents after a difficult vertex delivery with shoulder dystocia. The newborn's arm is held adducted, internally rotated at the shoulder, with the elbow extended and forearm pronated ("waiter's tip" deformity). Which nerve roots are damaged in this condition?
- A) C5, C6
- B) C8, T1
- C) C7, C8
- D) C5, C6, C7

**Answer:** A) C5, C6

**Explanation:**
**Erb's Palsy (Erb-Duchenne Palsy)** is an upper trunk brachial plexus lesion involving the **C5 and C6 nerve roots**. It commonly occurs due to excessive increase in the angle between the head and shoulder (e.g., birth trauma/shoulder dystocia or falling on the shoulder).

**Paralyzed Muscles & Clinical Features:**
1. **Supraspinatus & Infraspinatus (Suprascapular nerve C5-C6):** Loss of shoulder abduction & external rotation -> Arm is **adducted & internally rotated**.
2. **Biceps brachii & Brachialis (Musculocutaneous nerve C5-C6):** Loss of elbow flexion & forearm supination -> Elbow is **extended** and forearm is **pronated**.
3. **Brachioradialis & Subclavius:** Loss of auxiliary flexion and stabilization.

The classic appearance is the **"Waiter's Tip" (Policeman's Tip)** position: adducted arm, internally rotated shoulder, extended elbow, and pronated forearm. The Moro reflex is absent on the affected side, but finger grasp reflex remains intact (C8-T1 normal).

**Take Home Message:** Erb's palsy results from C5-C6 upper trunk brachial plexus injury, presenting as a "waiter's tip" deformity with adducted, internally rotated shoulder, extended elbow, and pronated forearm.
