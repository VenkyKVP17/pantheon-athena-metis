# General Anatomy QBank

## Q1. Epiphysis Types
**Question:** During skeletal growth, some secondary ossification centres are dragged into existence by the repeated pull of a powerful tendon, appearing at sites such as the greater trochanter of the femur and the tubercles of the humerus. An epiphysis that forms at such a site is classified as which type?
- A) Traction epiphysis
- B) Pressure epiphysis
- C) Aberrant epiphysis
- D) Atavistic epiphysis

**Answer:** A) Traction epiphysis

**Explanation:**
A traction epiphysis is pulled out at a tendon attachment (e.g., greater and lesser trochanters of the femur, tubercles of the humerus). 
Pressure epiphyses lie at weight-transmitting articular ends (femoral and humeral heads).
An aberrant epiphysis is normally absent (e.g., first metacarpal head).
An atavistic epiphysis refers to a bone that was independent in evolutionary ancestors and is now annexed to a neighbour (e.g., coracoid process, os trigonum).

**Take Home Message:** Tendon pull builds a traction epiphysis; joint loading builds a pressure epiphysis.

## Q2. Bony Landmarks and Epiphyses
**Question:** A trainee is sorting named bony landmarks into their epiphysis types. Decide which of the pairings below are correct.
1. The head of the femur is a pressure epiphysis.
2. The greater trochanter is a traction epiphysis.
3. The coracoid process is an aberrant epiphysis.
4. The head of the first metacarpal is a traction epiphysis.

**Answer:** 1 and 2 only.

**Explanation:**
- Head of the femur transmits body weight = pressure epiphysis.
- Greater trochanter receives pull of gluteal muscles = traction epiphysis.
- Coracoid process was a separate ossicle = atavistic (not aberrant).
- Head of the first metacarpal = aberrant (not traction).

**Take Home Message:** Coracoid process is atavistic and the first metacarpal head is aberrant; do not swap the two.

## Q3. Dermatomes
**Question:** A man with a thoracic spinal cord injury is examined for his sensory level. Sensation is preserved above the umbilicus but lost below it, placing the level exactly at the umbilicus. This corresponds to which dermatome?
- A) L1
- B) T10
- C) T7
- D) T4

**Answer:** B) T10

**Explanation:**
A sensory loss exactly at the umbilicus maps to the T10 cord segment. 
L1 supplies just above the inguinal ligament and pubic symphysis.
T7 maps to the xiphoid process.
T4 corresponds to the nipple line.

**Take Home Message:** Umbilicus is T10; nipple T4, xiphoid T7 and the groin L1 complete the trunk map.

## Q4. Anatomical Planes
**Question:** Anatomical planes and directional terms are used in every imaging report. All of the following statements are correct EXCEPT:
- A) The median sagittal plane divides the body into equal right and left halves.
- B) The transverse plane is horizontal and parallel to the ground.
- C) The coronal plane divides the body into upper and lower parts.

**Answer:** C) The coronal plane divides the body into upper and lower parts.

**Explanation:**
The coronal (frontal) plane is vertical and divides the body into anterior (front) and posterior (back) parts. It is the transverse plane that separates top from bottom. 

**Take Home Message:** Coronal separates front from back; transverse separates top from bottom; median sagittal gives equal halves.

## Q5. Standard Operating Positions
**Question:** The lists describe each patient in a standard operating position. Decide which position-to-use pairings below are correct.
1. Lithotomy position is used for obstetric and genitourinary procedures.
2. Prone position is preferred for spine and back surgery.
3. Lateral decubitus is the best position for ear surgery.
4. Supine position is used mainly for perineal procedures.

**Answer:** 1, 2 and 3 only.

**Explanation:**
- Lithotomy: lays patient on back with hips/knees flexed, used for perineum, obstetric, genitourinary.
- Prone: face down, access to spine/posterior trunk.
- Lateral decubitus: lying on one side, used for ear surgery.
- Supine: flat on back, default for cardiothoracic/abdominal. It is NOT for perineal exposure.

**Take Home Message:** Perineum needs lithotomy, not supine; prone is for the spine and lateral decubitus for the ear.

## Q6. Fontanelles
**Question ID:** Q4236072
**Question:** A healthy 18-month-old is brought for a check-up. The mother asks when the soft spot at the top of the front of the skull should disappear. Which fontanelle is she describing, and when does it normally close?
- A) Posterolateral (mastoid) fontanelle, by 12 months
- B) Anterior fontanelle, by 12 to 24 months
- C) Posterior fontanelle, by 3 months
- D) Anterolateral (sphenoidal) fontanelle, by 6 months

**Answer:** B) Anterior fontanelle, by 12 to 24 months

**Explanation:**
The large soft spot in the midline at the front of the vault is the anterior fontanelle, found at the bregma. It is the last to close (normally 12-24 months).
Mastoid (posterolateral) closes near end of 1st year.
Posterior fontanelle shuts by ~3 months.
Sphenoidal (anterolateral) closes at roughly 6 months.

**Take Home Message:** The anterior fontanelle (bregma) is the last to close, at 12 to 24 months; the others shut within the first year.

## Q8. Neck Rotation and Joints
**Question ID:** Q2576499
**Question:** A physiotherapist asks a patient to rotate the head so the chin swings toward each shoulder in turn, the movement used to signal no. The joint that chiefly produces this rotation of the head belongs to which class?
- A) Pivot joint
- B) Ellipsoid joint
- C) Plane synovial joint
- D) Saddle joint

**Answer:** A) Pivot joint

**Explanation:**
Turning the head left and right is rotation about a vertical axis, the signature movement of a pivot (trochoid) joint. The relevant joint is the atlanto-axial joint between the atlas (C1) and axis (C2), where the dens acts as a pivot.
Option B: The ellipsoid atlanto-occipital joint nods the head in the yes movement.
Option C: Plane synovial joints allow gliding, not wide rotation.
Option D: Saddle joints permit opposition rather than rotation about a vertical axis.

**Take Home Message:** Side-to-side no rotation is the atlanto-axial pivot; the yes nod is the atlanto-occipital ellipsoid joint.

## Q9. Ball-and-Socket Joints
**Question ID:** Q5941720
**Question:** Ball-and-socket joints allow movement around three axes. All of the following are ball-and-socket joints EXCEPT:
- A) First carpometacarpal joint
- B) Shoulder joint
- C) Hip joint
- D) Talocalcaneonavicular joint

**Answer:** A) First carpometacarpal joint

**Explanation:**
The first carpometacarpal joint (base of the thumb) is a saddle joint, not a ball-and-socket. 
The genuine ball-and-socket joints are the shoulder, hip, incudostapedial joint of the middle ear, and the talocalcaneonavicular joint. 
Ball-and-socket joints are multiaxial, whereas a saddle joint is biaxial.

**Take Home Message:** Shoulder, hip and talocalcaneonavicular are ball-and-socket; the first carpometacarpal joint of the thumb is a saddle joint.

## Q10. Thumb Opposition and Saddle Joints
**Question ID:** Q7358367
**Question:** A hand therapist demonstrates how the thumb can be brought across the palm to touch the little finger, a movement no other digit can perform. The joint at the base of the thumb that makes this opposition possible is classified as which type of synovial joint?
- A) Pivot joint
- B) Hinge joint
- C) Plane joint
- D) Saddle joint

**Answer:** D) Saddle joint

**Explanation:**
Opposition of the thumb depends on the first carpometacarpal joint, between the trapezium and the base of the first metacarpal. Its articular surfaces are interlocking like a rider in a saddle, which is why it is the classic saddle (sellar) joint. This biaxial shape lets the thumb flex-extend and abduct-adduct, and the combination of these with a little axial rotation produces opposition. 
Other saddle joints include the sternoclavicular joint and the incudomalleolar joint of the ear ossicles.

**Take Home Message:** Thumb opposition is the work of the saddle-shaped first carpometacarpal joint (trapezium to first metacarpal).

## Q11. Joint Classification by Tissue
**Question ID:** Q3282773
**Question:** Joints can be classified by the tissue that unites the two bones. Decide which of the statements below are correct.
1. The middle radioulnar union is a syndesmosis, a fibrous joint.
2. The peg-and-socket joint anchoring a tooth in its alveolus is a gomphosis.
3. The junction between the epiphysis and diaphysis of a growing long bone is a synchondrosis.
4. The intervertebral disc joint is a primary cartilaginous joint.

**Answer:** 1, 2 and 3 only

**Explanation:**
- A syndesmosis joins bones by a ligament or interosseous membrane, exactly the arrangement of the middle radioulnar union, so it is a fibrous joint.
- A gomphosis is a fibrous joint that fixes a tooth in its alveolus.
- A synchondrosis (primary cartilaginous joint) is hyaline cartilage uniting bones with no movement, like the epiphyseal growth plate.
- The intervertebral disc is a secondary cartilaginous joint or symphysis, separated by fibrocartilage, not a primary cartilaginous joint.

**Take Home Message:** Middle radioulnar syndesmosis, dental gomphosis and the growth-plate synchondrosis are correct; the intervertebral disc is a secondary cartilaginous (symphysis) joint.

## Q12. Cartilaginous Joints
**Question ID:** Q7429462
**Question:** Two cartilaginous joints are compared. One is built of pure hyaline cartilage, is immovable, and is found at the growing end of a long bone; the other has fibrocartilage between hyaline-capped bone ends, allows slight movement, and lies in the midline. The second joint described is best termed a:
- A) Synostosis
- B) Primary cartilaginous joint (synchondrosis)
- C) Secondary cartilaginous joint (symphysis)
- D) Syndesmosis

**Answer:** C) Secondary cartilaginous joint (symphysis)

**Explanation:**
- A **primary cartilaginous joint (synchondrosis)** is solid hyaline cartilage, permits no movement, is temporary because it later ossifies, and sits at a growing end (e.g., epiphyseal plate, first sternocostal joint). 
- A **secondary cartilaginous joint (symphysis)** has hyaline cartilage capping each bone end but a wedge of fibrocartilage between them, permits slight movement, is largely permanent, and characteristically lies in the median plane (e.g., pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs, manubriosternal and sacrococcygeal joints). 
The second description matches a symphysis.

**Take Home Message:** Synchondrosis is immovable midline-or-not hyaline at a growing end; a symphysis is slightly mobile midline fibrocartilage (pubic symphysis, discs).

## Q13. Secondary Cartilaginous Joints (Symphyses)
**Question ID:** Q2682859
**Question:** Secondary cartilaginous joints (symphyses) share a fibrocartilage union, slight mobility and a midline location. All of the following are secondary cartilaginous joints EXCEPT:
- A) Pubic symphysis
- B) Manubriosternal joint
- C) First carpometacarpal joint
- D) Intervertebral disc joint

**Answer:** C) First carpometacarpal joint

**Explanation:**
The first carpometacarpal joint is a synovial saddle joint with a joint cavity and free movement, so it is not a secondary cartilaginous joint. 
True symphyses share three features: fibrocartilage between bones, only slight movement, and median plane position.
Option A: Pubic symphysis unites two pubic bones in the midline through fibrocartilage. 
Option B: Manubriosternal joint is a midline fibrocartilage union that ossifies late in life. 
Option D: Intervertebral disc joint sandwiches fibrocartilage between vertebral bodies.

**Take Home Message:** Pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs and the manubriosternal joint are symphyses; the first carpometacarpal joint is synovial (saddle).

## Q14. Ligamentum Flavum and Lumbar Puncture
**Question ID:** Q1500541
**Question:** During a lumbar puncture the needle passes between adjacent vertebral laminae and gives a distinct give as it pierces a tough, yellow ligament just before reaching the epidural space. The high content of which fibre type gives this ligament its colour and recoil?
- A) Type I collagen fibres
- B) Elastic fibres
- C) Reticular fibres
- D) Type II collagen fibres

**Answer:** B) Elastic fibres

**Explanation:**
The ligament that gives the characteristic give during a lumbar puncture is the ligamentum flavum, which connects the laminae of adjacent vertebrae. Its name (flavum, yellow) reflects its high content of elastic fibres, which let it stretch as the spine flexes and recoil to its original length. 
In the midline lumbar approach, the needle traverses skin, superficial fascia, supraspinous and interspinous ligaments, then the ligamentum flavum, after which it enters the epidural space and finally the subarachnoid space. 
Contrast this with ligaments built mainly of type I collagen (like iliofemoral ligament) which are strong but barely stretchable.

**Take Home Message:** The ligamentum flavum is an elastic ligament; its recoil and yellow colour come from elastic fibres, and it is the last ligament pierced before the epidural space.

## Q15. Vertebral Column Curvatures
**Question ID:** Q3139924
**Question:** The adult vertebral column has four sagittal curvatures with different origins. Decide which of the statements below are correct.
1. The thoracic curvature is a primary curvature, present from fetal life.
2. The lumbar curvature is a secondary curvature that develops after birth.
3. The lumbar curvature is normally a lordosis.
4. The cervical curvature is a primary curvature.

**Answer:** 1, 2 and 3 only

**Explanation:**
Primary curvatures (thoracic and sacral) are concave forward (kyphotic) and are present from fetal life. 
Secondary curvatures (cervical and lumbar) are convex forward (lordotic) and are acquired after birth. Cervical lordosis appears when the baby begins to hold up the head. Lumbar lordosis appears when the baby sits and then stands and walks. 
The claim that the cervical curve is primary is wrong, it is secondary.

**Take Home Message:** Thoracic and sacral curves are primary (kyphotic); cervical and lumbar curves are secondary lordosis acquired after birth.

## Q16. Osteomyelitis Localisation
**Question ID:** Q6194491
**Question:** A 7-year-old develops acute osteomyelitis of the lower femur after a bloodstream infection. The infection characteristically lodges first at the metaphysis of the growing bone. Which feature of the metaphyseal blood supply best explains this localisation?
- A) Absence of any blood supply to the metaphysis
- B) A rich lymphatic plexus draining the growth plate
- C) Direct arterial supply from the overlying epiphysis
- D) Sluggish flow at the hairpin loops of the nutrient artery

**Answer:** D) Sluggish flow at the hairpin loops of the nutrient artery branches

**Explanation:**
In a growing child, the nutrient artery branches turn back on themselves in sharp hairpin loops before draining into wide venous sinusoids at the metaphysis. Blood flow slows at these loops, and bacteria delivered haematogenously settle and multiply there, which is why acute osteomyelitis classically begins in the metaphysis. 
The growth plate acts as a relative barrier, so infection stays on the metaphyseal side. The commonest organism is Staphylococcus aureus.

**Take Home Message:** Hairpin loops of the metaphyseal vessels slow blood flow and trap blood-borne bacteria, making the metaphysis the classic site of childhood osteomyelitis.

## Q17. Epiphyseal Growth Plate Zones
**Question ID:** Q4980516
**Question:** The epiphyseal growth plate is organised into zones from the resting cartilage toward the diaphysis. All of the following are recognised zones of the growth plate EXCEPT:
- A) Zone of proliferation
- B) Zone of fibrous metaplasia
- C) Zone of hypertrophy
- D) Zone of calcification

**Answer:** B) Zone of fibrous metaplasia

**Explanation:**
There is no fibrous metaplasia zone in the normal growth plate. The genuine zones (from epiphysis toward diaphysis) are: 
1. Resting (reserve or germinal) zone
2. Proliferative zone (cells divide and stack)
3. Hypertrophic zone (cells stop dividing and swell)
4. Zone of calcification (matrix calcifies)
5. Zone of ossification (bone laid down by osteoblasts).

**Take Home Message:** Growth-plate zones run resting, proliferative, hypertrophic, calcification, then ossification; there is no fibrous metaplasia zone.

## Q18. Shoulder Abduction
**Question ID:** Q7773113
**Question:** While examining the shoulder, a clinician asks the patient to raise the straight arm sideways away from the body until it is horizontal. The movement being tested is termed:
- A) Abduction
- B) Flexion
- C) Adduction
- D) Lateral rotation

**Answer:** A) Abduction

**Explanation:**
Movements are named relative to the anatomical position. Raising the limb away from the midline in the coronal plane is abduction; bringing it back toward the midline is adduction.
- Option B: Flexion at the shoulder swings the arm forward in the sagittal plane, decreasing the angle in front, not lifting it sideways. Flexion and extension occur in the sagittal plane.
- Option C: Adduction brings the limb back toward the midline, the reverse of the movement being demonstrated.
- Option D: Lateral rotation turns the front of the arm outward about its long axis without moving the whole arm away from the trunk.

**Take Home Message:** Movement away from the midline is abduction; toward the midline is adduction; forward in the sagittal plane is flexion. Circumduction is the cone-shaped combination of flexion, abduction, extension and adduction in sequence, as in bowling. Because the patient lifts the straight arm sideways to the horizontal, the movement is abduction, the first 15 degrees or so of which at the shoulder is initiated by supraspinatus before deltoid takes over.

## Q19. Muscle Architecture (Pennation)
**Question ID:** Q3985707
**Question:** Muscles are classified by the arrangement of their fascicles relative to the tendon. In a muscle whose fibres approach the central tendon from two sides like the barbs of a feather, the architecture is described as:
- A) Bipennate
- B) Fusiform
- C) Unipennate
- D) Strap (parallel)

**Answer:** A) Bipennate

**Explanation:**
In pennate (feather-like) muscles the fascicles attach obliquely to a central tendon, which packs more fibres into a given volume and increases force at the expense of range. A bipennate muscle has fibres converging from both sides like a feather (e.g., dorsal interossei, rectus femoris).
- Option B: A fusiform muscle is spindle-shaped with parallel fibres running to a tendon at each end, as in the biceps brachii.
- Option C: A unipennate muscle has fibres feeding the tendon from one side only, the arrangement of the palmar interossei.
- Option D: Strap muscles, such as the sternohyoid, have long parallel fibres and no feather-like pennation at all. Non-pennate types include parallel or strap muscles with quadrilateral muscles such as the rectus abdominis with its tendinous intersections.

**Take Home Message:** Bipennate fibres feed a central tendon from both sides (dorsal interossei); fusiform is spindle-shaped and strap is long and parallel. Multipennate muscle has several tendons with fibres between them (the middle fibres of deltoid, and subscapularis).

## Q20. Transpyloric Plane
**Question ID:** Q7687322
**Question:** A surgeon refers to the transpyloric plane when describing the surface projection of several upper abdominal structures. This horizontal plane lies at the level of which vertebra?
- A) Tenth thoracic vertebra
- B) First lumbar vertebra
- C) Third lumbar vertebra
- D) Fifth lumbar vertebra

**Answer:** B) First lumbar vertebra

**Explanation:**
The transpyloric plane of Addison is a horizontal plane midway between the jugular notch and the pubic symphysis, lying at the level of the first lumbar vertebra (its lower border). It is a busy surface landmark: the pylorus of the stomach (when supine), the neck of the pancreas, the fundus of the gallbladder, the origin of the superior mesenteric artery, the hila of the kidneys (the left a little higher), the duodenojejunal flexure, and the termination of the spinal cord in the adult all lie close to it. Because so many structures cluster at L1, the transpyloric plane is a favourite for relating a viscus to a vertebral level. 
Distinguish it from the subcostal plane (around L3) and the supracristal plane through the iliac crests (around L4), the last being the landmark for a lumbar puncture below the cord.

**Take Home Message:** The transpyloric plane lies at L1 and projects the pylorus, pancreatic neck, SMA origin and renal hila.

## Q21. Body Wall Layers & Neurovascular Bundles
**Question ID:** Q6340109
**Question:** A demonstrator describes the layered plan of the body wall and the contents of a neurovascular space. Decide which of the statements below are correct.
1. From the surface inward the layers run skin, superficial fascia, deep fascia, then muscle.
2. Cutaneous nerves and vessels travel in the superficial fascia.
3. By Hilton's law, the nerve that supplies a joint also supplies the muscles moving that joint and the skin over them.
4. A typical neurovascular bundle contains only an artery and a vein, with no nerve.

- A) 1, 2 and 3 only
- B) 1 and 4 only
- C) 2, 3 and 4 only

**Answer:** A) 1, 2 and 3 only

**Explanation:**
The body wall is built in layers that the dissector meets in order: skin, then superficial fascia (the fatty layer carrying cutaneous nerves and vessels), then the membranous deep fascia, then muscle, and finally bone. 
A neurovascular bundle running between or deep to muscles characteristically carries three structures together, a vein, an artery and a nerve, which is why the no-nerve statement is wrong. 
Hilton's law is the elegant clinical rule that the nerve supplying a joint also supplies the muscles that move that joint and the skin overlying their insertions, which is why hip pathology can refer pain to the knee (both served by the obturator and femoral nerves).

**Take Home Message:** Layers run skin, superficial fascia, deep fascia, muscle; a neurovascular bundle is vein, artery and nerve; Hilton's law links a joint's nerve to its muscles and overlying skin.

## Q22. Supination and Pronation Joints
**Question ID:** Q4459549
**Question:** Turning the palm from facing down to facing up (supination) and back (pronation) is produced at the joints between the radius and ulna. The superior and inferior radioulnar joints that drive this movement are which type of synovial joint?
- A) Plane joints
- B) Hinge joints
- C) Pivot joints
- D) Saddle joints

**Answer:** C) Pivot joints

**Explanation:**
Supination and pronation are rotations of the radius about a longitudinal axis, the work of pivot (trochoid) joints. 
- At the **superior radioulnar joint** the head of the radius spins within the ring formed by the radial notch of the ulna and the annular ligament. 
- At the **inferior radioulnar joint** the head of the ulna sits in the ulnar notch of the radius and the radius swings around it. 
Acting together, these two pivots roll the radius over the ulna so the hand turns palm-down or palm-up, while the middle radioulnar union (the interosseous membrane, a fibrous syndesmosis) holds the two bones together and transmits load. 
Note that the same pivot principle, axial rotation about a peg or ring, also defines the atlanto-axial joint at the top of the neck.

**Take Home Message:** Supination and pronation occur at the superior and inferior radioulnar pivot joints; the interosseous membrane between them is a fibrous syndesmosis.

## Q23. Hinge Joints (Uniaxial)
**Question ID:** Q4171028
**Question:** The synovial joint illustrated, with the curved arrows indicating the only movements it permits, belongs to which structural class? (Image shows an elbow joint with arrows showing flexion and extension).
- A) Pivot joint
- B) Ball-and-socket joint
- C) Saddle joint
- D) Hinge joint

**Answer:** D) Hinge joint

**Explanation:**
The elbow is the classic teaching example of a hinge joint, where the trochlea of the humerus grips the trochlear notch of the ulna to give a uniaxial articulation. The projecting bony margins restrict movement to flexion and extension, which is why injury here tends to limit the joint to that one plane. 
Other hinge joints in the standard list are the interphalangeal joints of the digits and the ankle (talocrural) joint. The defining feature of a hinge is uniaxial motion enforced by interlocking bony prominences.

**Take Home Message:** When a single curved arrow set marks movement in one plane only, think uniaxial: the elbow, the interphalangeal joints and the ankle are the hinge joints to recall.
