# Arrhythmias & Antiarrhythmics QBank

## Q1. Tachyarrhythmia Electrophysiological Mechanisms
**Question ID:** Q7600922
**Question:** Regarding the mechanisms behind fast heart rhythms (tachyarrhythmias), which of the following statements are correct?
1. Re-entry is the commonest mechanism.
2. Torsades de pointes is a form of triggered activity that arises from early afterdepolarizations.
3. Digoxin-toxic arrhythmias arise from enhanced normal automaticity.
4. A prolonged QT interval shortens the action potential duration.

- A) 1, 2, 3 and 4
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only
- D) 1 and 2 only

**Answer:** D) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Re-entry:* Accounts for ~80% of tachyarrhythmias. Requires 2 conduction pathways with different conduction velocities and refractory periods forming a circuit.
- *Early Afterdepolarizations (EADs):* Triggered activity during prolonged repolarization (long QT phase 3/4) $\rightarrow$ **Torsades de Pointes**.
- *Delayed Afterdepolarizations (DADs):* Triggered activity from intracellular calcium overload (phase 4) $\rightarrow$ **Digoxin toxicity** and **Catecholaminergic polymorphic VT**.
- *Long QT:* Represents **prolonged** action potential duration (not shortened).

**Take Home Message:** Re-entry drives most fast rhythms. Early afterdepolarizations cause long-QT torsades, while delayed afterdepolarizations cause digoxin-toxic arrhythmias.

## Q2. Amiodarone Organ Toxicity
**Question ID:** Q3795885
**Question:** A patient on long-term amiodarone is followed up for drug-related organ damage. Which of the following is NOT a recognised adverse effect of the drug?
- A) Gynecomastia
- B) Thyroid dysfunction
- C) Pulmonary fibrosis
- D) Corneal microdeposits

**Answer:** A) Gynecomastia

**Explanation:**
Amiodarone is an iodine-rich Class III antiarrhythmic with extensive multisystem organ toxicities:
1. **Lungs:** Pulmonary fibrosis / interstitial pneumonitis (most serious/fatal side effect).
2. **Thyroid:** Hypothyroidism (Amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism - AIH) or Hyperthyroidism (AIT) due to 37% iodine content.
3. **Eyes:** Corneal microdeposits (whorl keratopathy in >90%), optic neuritis/neuropathy.
4. **Skin:** Blue-grey slate skin discoloration and photosensitivity.
5. **Liver:** Transaminitis, hepatitis, cirrhosis.
6. **Nerves:** Peripheral neuropathy.
*Gynecomastia* is NOT associated with amiodarone (associated with Spironolactone, Digoxin, Ketoconazole, Cimetidine).

**Take Home Message:** Amiodarone harms the lungs, thyroid, liver, eyes, and skin.

## Q3. Unstable Tachycardia Management Algorithm
**Question ID:** Q7055840
**Question:** A 59-year-old woman comes in with palpitations. She is confused, her blood pressure is 84/50 mmHg, and she has a regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 195 beats per minute. What is the most appropriate immediate management?
- A) An intravenous amiodarone infusion
- B) Intravenous adenosine
- C) Synchronized DC cardioversion
- D) Oral metoprolol

**Answer:** C) Synchronized DC cardioversion

**Explanation:**
Any tachyarrhythmia with **haemodynamic instability** (SBP < 90 mmHg, shock, altered mental status, acute pulmonary edema, ongoing chest pain) requires **IMMEDIATE SYNCHRONIZED DC CARDIOVERSION**, regardless of whether QRS is narrow or wide. 
Synchronization aligns the shock with the R-wave to avoid shocking during the vulnerable T-wave (R-on-T phenomenon which causes VF).
*Energy levels:* Narrow regular (50-100 J), Wide regular (100 J), Narrow irregular (120-200 J).

**Take Home Message:** A fast rhythm with low blood pressure, shock, ischaemia, acute heart failure, or syncope means immediate synchronized cardioversion, whatever the rhythm.

## Q4. Stable Narrow-Complex SVT Management
**Question ID:** Q2070467
**Question:** A 26-year-old woman has sudden onset of palpitations. She is comfortable, her blood pressure is 118/76 mmHg, and she has a regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 185 beats per minute with no visible P waves. Vagal maneuvers fail. What is the next step?
- A) Intravenous adenosine
- B) Synchronized cardioversion
- C) Intravenous verapamil as the first drug
- D) Intravenous digoxin

**Answer:** A) Intravenous adenosine

**Explanation:**
Management of **Stable Regular Narrow-Complex SVT** (AVNRT / AVRT):
1. **1st Line:** Vagal maneuvers (Modified Valsalva maneuver or Carotid sinus massage).
2. **2nd Line (First Drug):** **IV Adenosine** rapid bolus (6 mg rapid push $\rightarrow$ 12 mg $\rightarrow$ 12 mg). Briefly blocks AV node to break re-entry loop.
3. **3rd Line:** IV Non-dihydropyridine CCB (Verapamil / Diltiazem) or IV Beta-blocker (Metoprolol / Esmolol).

**Take Home Message:** Stable regular narrow-complex supraventricular tachycardia: vagal maneuvers, then adenosine; cardioversion is for the unstable patient.

## Q5. Stable Wide-Complex Tachycardia (Ventricular Tachycardia)
**Question ID:** Q3340351
**Question:** A 66-year-old man with a previous MI has palpitations. He is alert, his blood pressure is 124/80 mmHg, and his rhythm strip is shown (Monomorphic Wide-Complex Tachycardia). What is the most appropriate treatment?
- A) Intravenous adenosine
- B) Intravenous amiodarone
- C) Intravenous verapamil
- D) Carotid sinus massage

**Answer:** B) Intravenous amiodarone

**Explanation:**
A regular wide-complex tachycardia ($\text{QRS} > 120\text{ ms}$) in a patient with structural heart disease (prior MI) MUST be assumed to be **Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)**.
- **Stable VT:** **IV Amiodarone** (150 mg IV over 10 min, then 1 mg/min infusion) or IV Procainamide / Sotalol.
- *CONTRAINDICATION:* **Verapamil / Diltiazem** or **Adenosine** given in VT can cause severe vasodilation, cardiac collapse, and ventricular fibrillation!

**Take Home Message:** A regular wide uniform tachycardia, especially after MI, is ventricular tachycardia; treat the stable patient with amiodarone, never with verapamil or adenosine.

## Q6. Torsades de Pointes (Polymorphic VT) Management
**Question ID:** Q3331749
**Question:** A patient taking a QT-prolonging drug has repeated blackouts, and the rhythm strip is shown. Which is the most appropriate immediate treatment?
- A) Intravenous magnesium sulphate
- B) Intravenous amiodarone
- C) Intravenous adenosine
- D) Intravenous digoxin

**Answer:** A) Intravenous magnesium sulphate

**Explanation:**
The rhythm strip demonstrates polymorphic ventricular tachycardia where the QRS complexes continuously twist in amplitude around the isoelectric baseline—a classic pattern of **Torsades de Pointes** (triggered by early afterdepolarizations in the setting of a prolonged QT interval).
- **First-Line Treatment:** **IV Magnesium Sulphate** (1 to 2 g IV over 20 to 60 minutes) given **irrespective of baseline serum magnesium levels**.
- **Co-Management:** Immediately withdraw all QT-prolonging medications, correct electrolyte deficits (hypokalemia, hypocalcemia), and consider overdrive pacing or Isoprenaline if recurrent.
- *CRITICAL CONTRAINDICATION:* **Amiodarone** prolongs the QT interval further and is **contraindicated** in Torsades de Pointes!

**Take Home Message:** This rhythm is treated with intravenous magnesium sulphate and withdrawal of QT-prolonging drugs.

## Q7. Atrial Fibrillation Bedside & ECG Features
**Question ID:** Q4115126
**Question:** A 68-year-old man has an irregularly irregular pulse of 128 beats per minute, but his radial pulse is only 104, and his jugular venous pulse has no a waves. Which is the most likely rhythm?
- A) Frequent ventricular ectopics on a sinus rhythm
- B) Atrial flutter with fixed atrioventricular block
- C) Multifocal atrial tachycardia
- D) Atrial Fibrillation

**Answer:** D) Atrial Fibrillation

**Explanation:**
Atrial Fibrillation (AF) is characterized by four hallmark features:
1. **Pulse:** Irregularly irregular rhythm.
2. **Pulse Deficit:** Apex heart rate (128 bpm) > Radial pulse rate (104 bpm) because weak, uncoordinated ventricular beats fail to open the aortic valve or project a peripheral impulse.
3. **JVP:** **Absent *a* wave** due to loss of coordinated atrial systole.
4. **ECG:** Absent P waves, replaced by fine fibrillatory *f* waves, with irregular QRS intervals.
*Clinical Note:* Left atrial appendage (LAA) is the primary site of thrombus formation in AF.

**Take Home Message:** An irregularly irregular pulse, no P waves, no a wave in the jugular pulse, and a pulse deficit together mean atrial fibrillation.

## Q8. Anticoagulation in AF (CHA2DS2-VASc vs HAS-BLED Criteria)
**Question ID:** Q9302980
**Question:** Which of the following statements about blood-thinning (anticoagulation) in atrial fibrillation are correct?
1. In non-valvular atrial fibrillation a direct oral anticoagulant is preferred over warfarin when stroke risk is high.
2. Moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis or a mechanical valve requires warfarin rather than a direct oral anticoagulant.
3. The decision to anticoagulate for stroke prevention is made using the HAS-BLED score.
4. After an ischemic stroke caused by atrial fibrillation, anticoagulation is contraindicated.

- A) 1, 2 and 3 only
- B) 1 and 2 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only

**Answer:** B) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Statement 1 & 2 are True:* In non-valvular AF, stroke risk is stratified using **CHA2DS2-VASc**. When anticoagulation is indicated ($\ge 2$ in men, $\ge 3$ in women), **Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)** (Apixaban, Dabigatran, Rivaroxaban) are preferred over Warfarin. Valvular AF (moderate-to-severe MS or mechanical valve) MANDATES **Warfarin**.
- *Statement 3 is False:* **CHA2DS2-VASc** determines whether to start anticoagulation. **HAS-BLED** gauges bleeding risk to address modifiable risk factors (it does NOT determine whether to withhold anticoagulation).
- *Statement 4 is False:* Ischemic stroke caused by AF is an absolute indication for long-term anticoagulation.

### 📋 CHA2DS2-VASc Scoring Breakdown Table

| Category / Component | Risk Factor | Points |
| :--- | :--- | :---: |
| **C** | Congestive Heart Failure / LV dysfunction | 1 |
| **H** | Hypertension | 1 |
| **A2** | Age $\ge 75$ years | **2** |
| **D** | Diabetes Mellitus | 1 |
| **S2** | Prior Stroke / TIA / Thromboembolism | **2** |
| **V** | Vascular Disease (prior MI, PAD, aortic plaque) | 1 |
| **A** | Age 65 to 74 years | 1 |
| **Sc** | Sex Category (Female) | 1 |

### 📊 Anticoagulation Thresholds:
- **Low Risk (0 Male, 1 Female):** No anticoagulation.
- **Intermediate Risk (1 Male, 2 Female):** Consider anticoagulation.
- **High Risk ($\ge 2$ Male, $\ge 3$ Female):** Anticoagulation Recommended (DOAC preferred in non-valvular AF).

**Take Home Message:** CHA2DS2-VASc decides anticoagulation and HAS-BLED gauges bleeding; valvular atrial fibrillation needs warfarin, non-valvular a direct oral anticoagulant.

## Q9. Pre-Excited Atrial Fibrillation (WPW + AF) Management
**Question ID:** Q8051976
**Question:** A patient with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome develops atrial fibrillation with a fast, irregular, broad-complex tachycardia. All of the following drugs are contraindicated EXCEPT:
- A) Adenosine
- B) Procainamide
- C) Digoxin
- D) Verapamil

**Answer:** B) Procainamide

**Explanation:**
In **pre-excited atrial fibrillation** (AF in the setting of Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome), impulses bypass the AV node and conduct rapidly down the accessory pathway (Bundle of Kent), producing an irregular, broad-complex tachycardia.
- **Contraindicated Drugs (Mnemonic: ABCD):**
  - **A**denosine
  - **B**eta-blockers
  - **C**alcium channel blockers (Verapamil, Diltiazem)
  - **D**igoxin
  *Reason:* Blocking the AV node removes the competing nodal route and forces ALL impulses down the fast accessory pathway, triggering **Ventricular Fibrillation**.
- **Drugs of Choice:** **Procainamide** or **Ibutilide** (slows accessory pathway conduction). If unstable $\rightarrow$ **Synchronized DC Cardioversion**. Definitive cure: Radiofrequency catheter ablation.

**Take Home Message:** In pre-excited atrial fibrillation avoid all AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin); use procainamide or cardioversion.

## Q10. Complete (Third-Degree) Heart Block & AV Block Classification
**Question ID:** Q9675335
**Question:** Identify the correct statements about complete (third-degree) heart block:
1. There is atrioventricular dissociation, with the atria and ventricles beating at independent rates.
2. It produces intermittent cannon a waves in the jugular venous pulse.
3. Atropine reliably restores conduction in infranodal complete block.
4. First-degree block, like complete block, needs a permanent pacemaker.

- A) 1, 2 and 3 only
- B) 1 and 2 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only

**Answer:** B) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Statement 1 & 2 are True:* Complete heart block is characterized by complete AV dissociation (P waves and QRS complexes beat independently) with a slow ventricular escape rate causing syncope (Stokes-Adams attacks). Contraction of the right atrium against a closed tricuspid valve produces **intermittent cannon *a* waves** in the JVP.
- *Statement 3 is False:* Atropine acts on the AV node (nodal level) and **fails** in infranodal complete block.
- *Statement 4 is False:* First-degree block (constant PR > 200 ms) is benign and does not require a pacemaker.

### 📋 AV Heart Block Types & Features Table

| Block Type | Key ECG Feature | Anatomical Level | Permanent Pacemaker (PPM)? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **First-Degree** | Constant PR > 200 ms; 1:1 P-to-QRS conduction | AV Node | **No** (Benign) |
| **Mobitz I (Wenckebach)** | Progressive PR lengthening until dropped QRS | AV Node | **No** (Unless symptomatic) |
| **Mobitz II** | Constant PR interval with intermittent dropped QRS | Infranodal (His-Purkinje) | **YES** (High risk of 3rd degree) |
| **Third-Degree (Complete)** | Complete AV dissociation; independent P and QRS | Infranodal / Nodal | **YES** (MANDATORY) |

**Take Home Message:** Complete heart block is atrioventricular dissociation with cannon a waves and blackouts, treated by pacing; atropine does not help an infranodal block.

## Q11. Symptomatic Bradycardia & Infranodal Block Protocol
**Question ID:** Q7265126
**Question:** A patient with symptomatic bradycardia from a Mobitz type II block (wide QRS) does not respond to intravenous atropine. What is the next step?
- A) Observation alone
- B) A further dose of atropine
- C) Intravenous adenosine
- D) Transcutaneous or transvenous pacing

**Answer:** D) Transcutaneous or transvenous pacing

**Explanation:**
- **Atropine Mechanics:** Atropine speeds conduction through the AV node, so it helps **nodal blocks** (sinus bradycardia, Mobitz I). It does NOT work for (and can worsen) **infranodal blocks** (Mobitz II or complete block with wide QRS).
- **Emergency Step:** When atropine fails or the block is infranodal, immediate **Transcutaneous Pacing (TCP)** is initiated as a bridge, or a heart-rate raising infusion (Adrenaline / Dopamine) while setting up **Transvenous Pacing**.
- **Definitive Step:** Mobitz II and complete heart block require a **Permanent Pacemaker (PPM)**.
- **Initial Step:** Discontinue all offending AV-nodal blocking drugs (Beta-blockers, CCBs, Digoxin, Amiodarone, Ivabradine).

**Take Home Message:** Atropine helps nodal blocks but not infranodal ones; a Mobitz II or complete block that fails atropine needs pacing, then a permanent pacemaker.

## Q12. Permanent Pacemaker (PPM) Indications & Selection Rules
**Question ID:** Q8268131
**Question:** Which of the following statements about permanent pacemakers are correct?
1. Symptomatic sinus node dysfunction is the commonest indication.
2. Mobitz type II and complete heart block warrant a pacemaker even when there are no symptoms.
3. A single-chamber ventricular pacemaker is preferred when sinus rhythm and atrial transport must be preserved.
4. First-degree atrioventricular block is itself an indication for a permanent pacemaker.

- A) 1, 2 and 3 only
- B) 1 and 2 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only

**Answer:** B) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Statement 1 is True:* **Symptomatic Sinus Node Dysfunction** (Sick Sinus Syndrome) is the single most common indication for a permanent pacemaker (PPM).
- *Statement 2 is True:* **Mobitz II** and **Complete (3rd Degree) Heart Block** warrant a permanent pacemaker EVEN IF ASYMPTOMATIC due to the severe risk of sudden cardiac death.
- *Statement 3 is False:* A **Dual-Chamber Pacemaker (DDD)** is preferred when sinus rhythm and AV synchrony / atrial transport ("atrial kick") must be preserved to prevent "pacemaker syndrome". Single-chamber ventricular pacing (VVI) is used in permanent AF with AV block.
- *Statement 4 is False:* First-degree block is benign and not an indication.
- *Other Indications:* Carotid sinus hypersensitivity (pauses $\ge 3$ sec), congenital complete block (maternal anti-Ro/SSA lupus), neuromuscular disease with conduction block.

**Take Home Message:** Symptomatic sinus-node disease tops the pacemaker indications; Mobitz II and complete block qualify even when silent, but first-degree block does not.

## Q13. Adult Cardiac Arrest (ACLS) Shockable vs Non-Shockable Protocol
**Question ID:** Q4247278
**Question:** Which of the following statements about managing an adult cardiac arrest are correct?
1. The compression-to-ventilation ratio without an advanced airway is 30:2.
2. Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia are treated with immediate unsynchronized defibrillation.
3. Asystole is treated with immediate defibrillation.
4. Adrenaline is given only in shockable rhythms.

- A) 1, 2, 3 and 4
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only
- D) 1 and 2 only

**Answer:** D) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Statement 1 is True:* Basic Life Support (BLS) compression-to-ventilation ratio is **30:2** in adults without an advanced airway.
- *Statement 2 is True:* **Shockable Rhythms** = **Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)** & **Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT)**. Treated with **immediate UNSYNCHRONIZED Defibrillation**, followed by CPR, Adrenaline every 3-5 min (after 2nd shock), and Amiodarone 300 mg (after 3rd shock).
- *Statement 3 is False:* **Non-Shockable Rhythms** = **Asystole** & **Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA)**. They are NEVER shocked! Managed with immediate CPR + Adrenaline 1 mg IV ASAP (every 3-5 min).
- *Statement 4 is False:* Adrenaline is administered in BOTH shockable and non-shockable cardiac arrest protocols.

**Take Home Message:** Shock only ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia; asystole and pulseless electrical activity get compressions and adrenaline, never a shock.

## Q14. Hyperkalemia ECG Changes & Stepwise Management Algorithm
**Question ID:** Q1739798
**Question:** A dialysis patient who has missed sessions has tall, peaked T waves, a widening QRS, and flattened P waves on the ECG. Which abnormality is responsible, and what is the most urgent treatment?
- A) Hyperkalemia; intravenous calcium gluconate
- B) Hypokalemia; potassium replacement
- C) Hypercalcemia; saline and a bisphosphonate
- D) Hyponatremia; hypertonic saline

**Answer:** A) Hyperkalemia; intravenous calcium gluconate

**Explanation:**

### 📋 Sequential ECG Progression of Hyperkalemia:
1. **$K^+ 5.5 - 6.5 \text{ mEq/L}$:** Tall, narrow-based, peaked (tented) T waves.
2. **$K^+ 6.5 - 7.0 \text{ mEq/L}$:** PR interval prolongation, QRS widening, P wave flattening/loss.
3. **$K^+ > 7.0 - 9.0 \text{ mEq/L}$:** Sinoatrial nodal conduction lost, **sine-wave morphology** $\rightarrow$ Ventricular Fibrillation / Asystole.

### 💉 Stepwise Emergency Management of Hyperkalemia:
1. **Step 1 — Cardioprotection (Membrane Stabilization):** **IV Calcium Gluconate (10%)** or Calcium Chloride. *Does NOT lower serum potassium*, but antagonizes potassium's membrane depolarizing effect to prevent fatal arrhythmia (buys 30-60 mins).
2. **Step 2 — Shift $K^+$ Intracellularly (Redistribution):**
   - **IV Insulin (10 units regular) + Dextrose (50ml 50%)**.
   - **Nebulized Salbutamol (10-20 mg)** (Beta-2 agonist).
   - **IV Sodium Bicarbonate** (if metabolic acidosis coexists).
3. **Step 3 — Eliminate $K^+$ from Body (Definitive Excretion):**
   - **Hemodialysis** (fastest & definitive in ESRD/dialysis patients).
   - **Loop Diuretics** (Furosemide).
   - **Cation Exchange Binders** (Sodium Zirconium Cyclosilicate / Patiromer).

*Contrast:* **Hypokalemia** causes U waves, ST depression, and flattened/inverted T waves.

**Take Home Message:** Tall tented T waves with a widening QRS mean a high potassium; give intravenous calcium first to protect the heart, then shift and remove the potassium.

## Q15. ESC 2024 AF Rate Control Algorithm by Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF)
**Question ID:** Q5651744
**Question:** A patient with atrial fibrillation with HFrEF 40% needs the ventricular rate brought down despite being on metoprolol. Which agent is the next best step?
- A) Intravenous amiodarone
- B) Intravenous diltiazem
- C) Intravenous verapamil
- D) Intravenous digoxin

**Answer:** D) Intravenous digoxin

**Explanation:**

### 📊 ESC 2024 Guidelines for AF Rate Control:

| Baseline LVEF Status | First-Line Options | Second-Line / Combination Therapy | Absolute Contraindications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **LVEF > 40% (Preserved LVEF)** | Beta-blocker OR Non-DHP CCB (Verapamil / Diltiazem) OR Digoxin | Combination of Beta-blocker + Diltiazem/Verapamil or Digoxin | None |
| **LVEF $\le$ 40% (HFrEF / Heart Failure)** | **Beta-blocker AND/OR Digoxin** | Combination of Beta-blocker + Digoxin | **Non-DHP CCBs (Verapamil & Diltiazem)** |

- **Why Verapamil & Diltiazem are Contraindicated in HFrEF:** Non-dihydropyridine CCBs exert **potent negative inotropic effects**, worsening LV dysfunction and precipitating acute pulmonary edema / heart failure collapse!
- **Amiodarone:** Last resort for rate control (primary role is rhythm control; high long-term toxicity).

**Take Home Message:** Beta-blockers and non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers rate-control most atrial fibrillation, but in decompensated heart failure use digoxin to avoid worsening the failure.

## Q16. Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) Accessory Pathway (Bundle of Kent) Anatomy & ECG
**Question ID:** Q1616076
**Question:** All of the following are true of the accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) in Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome EXCEPT:
- A) It produces a delta wave and a short PR interval on the resting ECG.
- B) It conducts faster than the atrioventricular node and lacks decremental conduction.
- C) It is protected by the atrioventricular node from rapid atrial rates.
- D) It forms one limb of the re-entry circuit in atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia.

**Answer:** C) It is protected by the atrioventricular node from rapid atrial rates.

**Explanation:**
- **Bundle of Kent Characteristics:**
  1. Direct muscular bridge connecting atrium and ventricle, completely **bypassing the AV node**.
  2. Lacks rate-slowing, **decremental conduction** (conducts fast and is **UNPROTECTED** against rapid atrial impulses).
  3. Resting ECG Triad: **Short PR interval (<120 ms)**, **Delta wave** (slurred initial QRS upstroke), and **Wide QRS (>120 ms)**.
  4. Forms the re-entry circuit limb in **AVRT** (Atrioventricular Re-entrant Tachycardia).
- *Why Statement C is False:* The accessory pathway is NOT protected by the AV node. In AF, rapid impulses pass 1:1 down the accessory pathway into the ventricles (pre-excited AF), degenerating into Ventricular Fibrillation.
- **Definitive Cure:** Radiofrequency Catheter Ablation of the pathway.

**Take Home Message:** The Wolff-Parkinson-White accessory pathway conducts fast without rate-slowing protection, so atrial fibrillation can reach the ventricle at a dangerous rate.

## Q17. Tricyclic Antidepressant (TCA) Overdose ECG Signs & Sodium Bicarbonate Therapy
**Question ID:** Q3345848
**Question:** A patient who has overdosed on a tricyclic antidepressant has low blood pressure, a widened QRS, and a prominent terminal R wave in lead aVR. Which is the most appropriate treatment?
- A) Intravenous magnesium sulfate
- B) Intravenous calcium gluconate
- C) Intravenous sodium bicarbonate
- D) Intravenous potassium chloride

**Answer:** C) Intravenous sodium bicarbonate

**Explanation:**
- **Toxicity Mechanism:** TCAs (e.g., Amitriptyline, Imipramine) block **fast myocardial sodium channels** (quinidine-like Class Ia antiarrhythmic action), slowing Phase 0 depolarization and intraventricular conduction.
- **ECG Signature of TCA Toxicity:**
  1. **QRS Widening (>100 ms):** $>100\text{ ms}$ predicts seizures; $>160\text{ ms}$ predicts VT/VF.
  2. **Prominent Terminal R Wave in lead aVR ($R_{\text{aVR}}$):** $R_{\text{aVR}} > 3\text{ mm}$ or $R/S \text{ ratio in aVR} > 0.7$ (due to rightward terminal QRS axis shift).
  3. Long QTc interval.
- **First-Line Antidote / Management:** **Intravenous Sodium Bicarbonate ($1 - 2 \text{ mEq/kg}$ IV bolus)**.
  - *Mechanism:* Serum alkalization (pH 7.45 - 7.55) unbinds the drug from sodium channels; increased extracellular $\text{Na}^+$ concentration overcomes competitive channel blockade.

**Take Home Message:** A wide QRS with a terminal R wave in aVR after tricyclic overdose signals sodium-channel blockade; treat with sodium bicarbonate.

## Q18. Drug-Induced QT Prolongation & Antihistamines Risk
**Question ID:** Q6853724
**Question:** A patient on a macrolide antibiotic is also taking an antihistamine and is found to have a long QT interval. Which antihistamine, combined with the macrolide, most raises the risk of QT prolongation and torsades de pointes?
- A) Fexofenadine
- B) Cetirizine
- C) Loratadine
- D) Astemizole

**Answer:** D) Astemizole

**Explanation:**
- **High-Risk Older Antihistamines:** **Astemizole** and **Terfenadine** block cardiac hERG potassium channels. When combined with CYP3A4 inhibitors (Macrolides like Erythromycin/Clarithromycin, Azole antifungals, Grapefruit juice), their serum levels surge, causing severe **QT Prolongation & Torsades de Pointes** (hence withdrawn).
- **Cardiac-Safe Antihistamines:** **Fexofenadine** (active metabolite engineered without hERG blocking activity), **Cetirizine**, and **Loratadine** do not prolong QTc meaningfully.
- **Other Notable QT-Prolonging Drugs:** Class IA & III antiarrhythmics, Haloperidol, Methadone, Fluoroquinolones.

**Take Home Message:** Astemizole and terfenadine prolong the QT and trigger torsades when combined with enzyme inhibitors such as macrolides; the newer antihistamines are cardiac-safe.

## Q20. Jugular Venous Pulse (JVP) Waves & Clinical Abnormalities
**Question ID:** Q9100324
**Question:** Which of the following statements about the jugular venous pulse are correct?
1. The a wave is produced by right atrial contraction and follows the P wave.
2. The a wave is absent in atrial fibrillation.
3. Giant v waves are characteristic of tricuspid stenosis.
4. Cannon a waves are seen continuously in first-degree atrioventricular block.

- A) 1, 2 and 4 only
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1 and 2 only

**Answer:** C) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Statement 1 & 2 are True:* The **a wave** represents presystolic right atrial contraction (occurs after P wave on ECG). In **Atrial Fibrillation**, loss of organized atrial contraction causes **complete disappearance of the *a* wave**.
- *Statement 3 is False:* **Giant *v* waves** represent **Tricuspid Regurgitation** (systolic backflow into RA). Tricuspid Stenosis presents with a **large *a* wave**.
- *Statement 4 is False:* **Cannon *a* waves** are INTERMITTENT (occurring when RA contracts against a closed tricuspid valve during AV dissociation in complete block or VT), not a feature of 1st-degree block.

### 📋 Jugular Venous Pulse (JVP) Abnormality Master Table:

| JVP Wave Abnormality | Clinical Condition / Cause |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Absent *a* wave** | **Atrial Fibrillation** |
| **Large / Prominent *a* wave** | **Tricuspid Stenosis**, Pulmonary Hypertension, Pulmonary Stenosis |

| **Giant *v* wave** | **Tricuspid Regurgitation** |

| **Steep / Prominent *y* descent** | **Constrictive Pericarditis** (Friedreich sign), Tricuspid Regurgitation |

## Q21. Antiarrhythmic Drug-Specific Toxicities (Vaughan-Williams Classes)
**Question ID:** Q8083651

## Q33. Adenosine Pharmacology, Indications & Contraindications
**Question ID:** Q4897348

## Q39. Mobitz Type I (Wenckebach) AV Block Features

## Q40. Third-Degree (Complete) Heart Block Diagnostic Features

**Question:** An 80-year-old woman reports dizziness and near-fainting. Her pulse is 38 per minute. The rhythm strip shown is recorded. Which conduction abnormality best explains the findings?

**Explanation:**
- **Diagnostic Hallmark:** **Atrioventricular Dissociation**—P waves occur at a regular sinus rate (P-P constant) and QRS complexes occur at a regular escape rate (R-R constant), but P waves and QRS complexes are completely independent with no fixed PR interval.
- **Escape Rates:**
  - **Junctional Escape:** Narrow QRS, rate $40 - 60\text{ bpm}$.
  - **Ventricular Escape:** Wide QRS, rate $< 40\text{ bpm}$ (leads to severe bradycardia, dizziness, and Stokes-Adams syncope).
- **Treatment:** Permanent Pacemaker (PPM) implantation.

**Take Home Message:** Atrioventricular dissociation, where P waves and QRS complexes are independent, with a slow escape rhythm is complete (third-degree) heart block; it causes syncope (Stokes-Adams) and needs a permanent pacemaker.

## Q41. Bundle Branch Block Diagnostic Criteria (RBBB vs LBBB)
**Question ID:** Q7790367
**Question:** A 70-year-old man has the 12-lead ECG shown as part of a pre-operative check. The QRS duration is broad at about 130 ms. Which conduction abnormality is present?
- A) Right bundle branch block
- B) Left bundle branch block
- C) Left anterior fascicular block
- D) Wolff-Parkinson-White pre-excitation

**Answer:** A) Right bundle branch block

**Explanation:**

### 📋 Bundle Branch Block (RBBB vs LBBB) Diagnostic Criteria Table:

| Feature | Right Bundle Branch Block (RBBB) | Left Bundle Branch Block (LBBB) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **QRS Width** | Broad ($\ge 120\text{ ms}$) | Broad ($\ge 120\text{ ms}$) |
| **V1 Morphology** | **$RSR'$ ("rabbit-ear" / M-shaped complex)** with T wave inversion | **Deep, broad QS or rS wave** |
| **Leads I & V6 Morphology** | **Wide, slurred S wave** | **Broad, notched M-shaped R wave** |
| **Mnemonic** | **M-A-R-R-O-W** (M in V1, W in V6) | **W-I-L-L-I-A-M** (W in V1, M in V6) |
| **Clinical Causes** | PE, Cor pulmonale, ASD, Ischemia, Normal variant | **Always structural heart disease** (Ischemia, Cardiomyopathy, HTN) |

**Take Home Message:** A broad QRS with an RSR' (rabbit-ear) complex in V1 and a wide slurred S in I and V6 is right bundle branch block; left bundle branch block is the mirror image and usually signals structural heart disease.

## Q42. Atrial Fibrillation ECG Diagnostic Triad
**Question ID:** Q7835810
**Question:** A 72-year-old woman with palpitations and no chest pain has the 12-lead ECG shown. Which arrhythmia does the tracing most likely show?
- A) Atrial fibrillation
- B) Atrial flutter
- C) Sinus tachycardia
- D) Multifocal atrial tachycardia

**Answer:** A) Atrial fibrillation

**Explanation:**
- **Atrial Fibrillation Diagnostic Triad:**
  1. **Absence of P waves** (loss of organized atrial activity).
  2. **Irregularly irregular ventricular response** (unpredictable R-R intervals).
  3. **Fine chaotic fibrillatory baseline (*f* waves)**.

### 📋 Supraventricular Tachycardia Differential Diagnosis Table:

| Arrhythmia | P-Wave Appearance | Rhythm Regularity | Classic Atrial / Ventricular Rate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Atrial Fibrillation** | **Absent (fibrillatory baseline)** | **Irregularly irregular** | Atrial $> 350\text{ bpm}$; Ventricular variable |
| **Atrial Flutter** | **Sawtooth F waves (II, III, aVF)** | **Regular** (with 2:1 block) | Atrial $\sim 300\text{ bpm}$; Ventricular $\sim 150\text{ bpm}$ |
| **Multifocal Atrial Tachycardia** | **$\ge 3$ different P wave shapes** | **Irregularly irregular** | Ventricular $> 100\text{ bpm}$ (COPD/Hypoxia) |
| **AVNRT / AVRT (PSVT)** | Retrograde / Pseudo-S in V1 | **Strictly regular** | Ventricular $150-220\text{ bpm}$ |

**Take Home Message:** Absent P waves with a chaotic baseline and an irregularly irregular ventricular response is atrial fibrillation.
