## Q1. JVP Waveform in Cardiac Tamponade vs Constrictive Pericarditis
**Question ID:** Q9647251
**Question:** A patient with muffled heart sounds, low blood pressure, and a high jugular venous pressure has cardiac tamponade. Which jugular venous waveform fits this picture?
- A) A prominent y descent with a pericardial knock
- B) A prominent x descent with a blunted or absent y descent
- C) Large v waves with an absent x descent
- D) Intermittent cannon a waves

**Answer:** B) A prominent x descent with a blunted or absent y descent

**Explanation:**
Tamponade squeezes the heart throughout diastole, so the early-diastolic **y descent** (right ventricular filling upon tricuspid valve opening) is **blunted or absent**, while the systolic **x descent** remains prominent. 
In contrast, **constrictive pericarditis** allows early rapid filling that stops abruptly, producing **prominent x AND y descents**, a pericardial knock, and Kussmaul sign. 
Tamponade classic features include Beck's triad (muffled sounds, low BP, raised JVP), pulsus paradoxus, electrical alternans, and diastolic collapse of RA/RV on echo.

**Take Home Message:** Tamponade blunts the y descent (prominent x); constrictive pericarditis shows prominent x and y descents with Kussmaul sign and a knock.

## Q2. Clinical Features of Cardiac Tamponade
**Question ID:** Q4861198
**Question:** All of the following are features of cardiac tamponade EXCEPT:
- A) Pulsus paradoxus with an inspiratory systolic pressure fall greater than 10 mmHg
- B) A loud first heart sound
- C) Electrical alternans on the ECG
- D) Equalization of diastolic pressures across the cardiac chambers

**Answer:** B) A loud first heart sound

**Explanation:**
Heart sounds in tamponade are **muffled/distant**, NOT loud (loud S1 points to pliable mitral stenosis). 
Key tamponade findings: Beck's triad (muffled sounds, hypotension, distended neck veins), pulsus paradoxus (>10 mmHg inspiratory SBP drop), low-voltage QRS with electrical alternans (heart swinging in fluid), water-bottle heart silhouette, blunted y descent, and equalization of diastolic pressures across all 4 cardiac chambers.

**Take Home Message:** Tamponade muffles the heart sounds and shows pulsus paradoxus, electrical alternans, and a blunted y descent; relieve it with pericardiocentesis.

## Q3. Infective Endocarditis Organisms
**Question ID:** Q6471501
**Question:** A patient develops infective endocarditis 2 years after a prosthetic valve replacement. Which organism is the most likely cause?
- A) Staphylococcus aureus
- B) Coagulase-negative staphylococci
- C) Viridans streptococci
- D) Streptococcus gallolyticus

**Answer:** C) Viridans streptococci

**Explanation:**
- **Late prosthetic valve endocarditis (>=12 months post-op):** Behaves like native valve endocarditis; **Viridans streptococci** predominate.
- **Early prosthetic valve endocarditis (<12 months post-op):** Nosocomial; **Coagulase-negative staphylococci (Staph epidermidis)** predominate, followed by Staph aureus.
- **Native valve acute IE:** *Staph aureus* (especially IV drug users).
- **Native valve subacute IE:** *Viridans streptococci* (post-dental procedures).
- **Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis):** Strongly associated with colorectal carcinoma (prompts colonoscopy).

**Take Home Message:** Early prosthetic valve endocarditis is staphylococcal; late prosthetic disease (after 1 year) resembles native disease with viridans streptococci.

## Q4. Acute Rheumatic Fever & Modified Jones Criteria
**Question ID:** Q8408265
**Question:** Regarding acute rheumatic fever and the modified Jones criteria, which statements are true?
1. The major criteria are carditis, arthritis, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, and Sydenham chorea.
2. Arthritis is the most common major manifestation.
3. A prolonged PR interval is a major criterion.
4. The diagnosis can be made on two minor criteria alone, without evidence of streptococcal infection.

- A) 1, 2 and 4 only
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1 and 2 only

**Answer:** C) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- **Major criteria (JONES):** Carditis, Migratory polyarthritis (most common), Subcutaneous nodules, Erythema marginatum, Sydenham chorea.
- **Minor criteria (PEACE):** Prolonged PR interval, Raised ESR/CRP, Arthralgia, Fever. (Prolonged PR is minor, NOT major).
- **Diagnostic requirement:** Proof of preceding GAS infection (ASO / anti-DNase B title or positive culture) PLUS either **2 Major** OR **1 Major + 2 Minor** criteria. Minor criteria alone cannot establish diagnosis.
- *Pathognomonic lesion:* Aschoff body (most often damages mitral valve).

**Take Home Message:** Rheumatic fever needs streptococcal evidence plus two major or one major and two minor criteria; a prolonged PR interval is minor, and the Aschoff body is pathognomonic.

## Q5. Mitral Stenosis Physical Signs & Imaging
**Question ID:** Q2782537
**Question:** Which of the following statements about mitral stenosis are correct?
1. A loud first heart sound and an opening snap occur when the valve is still pliable.
2. Left ventricular enlargement is the earliest chamber change on the chest radiograph.
3. A shorter interval between the second heart sound and the opening snap indicates more severe stenosis.
4. The loud first heart sound is characteristically lost in mild mitral stenosis.

- A) 1, 2, 3 and 4
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only
- D) 1 and 3 only

**Answer:** D) 1 and 3 only

**Explanation:**
1. *Statement 1 is True:* Pliable valve produces loud S1, opening snap (OS), and mid-diastolic rumble.
2. *Statement 2 is False:* **Left atrium (LA)**, NOT left ventricle, is the first chamber to enlarge on CXR (straight left border, double density sign, splayed carina).
3. *Statement 3 is True:* Severity correlates inversely with S2-OS interval: a shorter A2-OS gap (and longer diastolic murmur) indicates tighter stenosis.
4. *Statement 4 is False:* Loud S1 is present in mild pliable disease and lost only when valve calcifies/rigidifies.

**Take Home Message:** A shorter second-sound-to-opening-snap interval and a longer murmur mean tighter mitral stenosis; the left atrium enlarges first on the chest film.

## Q6. Austin-Flint Murmur in Severe Aortic Regurgitation
**Question ID:** Q7832066
**Question:** A patient with chronic aortic regurgitation has a soft, low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at the apex, but the first heart sound is normal and there is no opening snap. What does this finding mean?
- A) Mitral regurgitation
- B) Coexisting mitral stenosis
- C) Severe aortic regurgitation (an Austin-Flint murmur)
- D) Tricuspid stenosis

**Answer:** C) Severe aortic regurgitation (an Austin-Flint murmur)

**Explanation:**
An apical mid-diastolic rumble in aortic regurgitation, without the loud S1 or opening snap of mitral stenosis, is an **Austin-Flint murmur** and points to severe Aortic Regurgitation. It occurs because the regurgitant jet from the aorta strikes the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve and partially closes the mitral orifice in diastole, mimicking mitral stenosis. 
Unlike true mitral stenosis, an Austin-Flint murmur requires no mitral valve intervention and resolves once the aortic valve is repaired/replaced.

**Take Home Message:** An apical mid-diastolic rumble in aortic regurgitation without a loud first sound or opening snap is an Austin-Flint murmur, a sign of severe regurgitation, not mitral stenosis.

## Q7. Aortic Stenosis Triad & Prognosis
**Question ID:** Q6659316
**Question:** A patient with severe aortic stenosis has a slow-rising, low-volume pulse and an ejection systolic murmur that radiates to the carotids. Once it appears, which symptom signals the shortest survival?
- A) Palpitations
- B) Angina
- C) Syncope
- D) Heart failure (dyspnea)

**Answer:** D) Heart failure (dyspnea)

**Explanation:**
The cardinal symptom triad of Aortic Stenosis (Mnemonic: **SAD / ASD**):
1. **Exertional Angina:** ~5 years average survival
2. **Exertional Syncope:** ~3 years average survival
3. **Exertional Dyspnea / Heart Failure:** ~2 years average survival (worst prognosis).
The appearance of any of these symptoms mandates prompt aortic valve replacement (AVR/TAVI). 
Severe AS Echo criteria: Valve area < 1.0 cm², Peak jet velocity > 4 m/sec, Mean gradient > 40 mmHg. Pulse is *pulsus parvus et tardus*.

**Take Home Message:** In aortic stenosis, angina runs about five years, syncope three, and heart failure two; any symptom mandates valve replacement, and heart failure is the most ominous.

## Q8. Infective Endocarditis (Modified Duke Criteria 2023)
**Question ID:** Q1988892
**Question:** All of the following statements about infective endocarditis are correct EXCEPT:
- A) Osler nodes and Roth spots are immunologic phenomena.
- B) Janeway lesions are painless vascular (embolic) phenomena.
- C) Splenomegaly is a common finding.
- D) A vegetation on echocardiography is a minor Duke criterion.

**Answer:** D) A vegetation on echocardiography is a minor Duke criterion.

**Explanation:**
A vegetation on imaging is a **MAJOR** Duke criterion, not a minor one. 

**Modified Duke Criteria (2023) Breakdown:**
- **Major Criteria:**
  1. *Microbiologic:* Typical organism from >=2 separate blood culture sets (S. aureus, Viridans group, S. lugdunensis, Enterococcus faecalis, HACEK).
  2. *Imaging:* Echocardiogram/CT/PET-CT showing Vegetation, Abscess, Valve Perforation/Aneurysm, or new Prosthetic Dehiscence.
  3. *Surgical:* Direct inspection during cardiac surgery.
- **Minor Criteria:**
  1. *Predisposition:* Previous IE, prosthetic valve, structural heart disease, IVDU.
  2. *Fever:* Temp > 38.0 °C.
  3. *Vascular Phenomena:* Major emboli, septic infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, Janeway lesions (painless).
  4. *Immunologic Phenomena:* Osler nodes (painful), Roth spots, Glomerulonephritis, Rheumatoid Factor.
  5. *Microbiologic Evidence:* Positive blood culture not meeting major criteria.
- **Diagnostic Categories:**
  - *Definite IE:* 2 Major, OR 1 Major + 3 Minor, OR 5 Minor.
  - *Possible IE:* 1 Major + 1 Minor, OR 3 Minor.

**Take Home Message:** A vegetation on imaging is a major Duke criterion; Osler nodes are painful and immune, Janeway lesions painless and embolic.

## Q9. Acute Pericarditis ECG & Clinical Features
**Question ID:** Q8140031
**Question:** A 35-year-old man has sharp central chest pain that is worse lying flat and eased by sitting forward, a few days after a viral illness; a scratchy sound is heard at the left sternal border. Which ECG finding is expected?
- A) ST elevation confined to leads II, III, and aVF
- B) Diffuse concave (saddle-shaped) ST elevation with PR-segment depression
- C) Deep symmetric T-wave inversions in V1 to V3
- D) Electrical alternans

**Answer:** B) Diffuse concave (saddle-shaped) ST elevation with PR-segment depression

**Explanation:**
Acute pericarditis features sharp pleuritic chest pain (worse lying flat, better sitting forward), pericardial friction rub, and classic ECG changes: **widespread concave (saddle-shaped) ST elevation** with **PR segment depression** across most leads (and PR elevation with ST depression in aVR). 
First-line treatment: NSAIDs + Colchicine.

**Take Home Message:** Pleuritic pain relieved by sitting forward with a rub and diffuse concave ST elevation plus PR depression is acute pericarditis; treat with a non-steroidal drug and colchicine.

## Q10. Constrictive Pericarditis
**Question ID:** Q9558672
**Question:** A patient has fatigue, peripheral edema, ascites, and a jugular venous pressure that rises on inspiration, with an early diastolic sound but no murmur. What is the diagnosis?
- A) Tricuspid regurgitation
- B) Cardiac tamponade
- C) Constrictive pericarditis
- D) Dilated cardiomyopathy

**Answer:** C) Constrictive pericarditis

**Explanation:**
Constrictive pericarditis is characterized by:
1. **Kussmaul sign:** Inspiratory rise in JVP (stiff pericardium prevents right heart from accommodating increased venous return).
2. **Pericardial knock:** Early diastolic sound caused by sudden arrest of ventricular filling.
3. **JVP Waveform:** Prominent *x* and *y* descents.
4. **Right heart failure:** Ascites, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly out of proportion to dyspnea.
Tuberculosis is the classic cause worldwide.

**Take Home Message:** Right-heart failure with Kussmaul sign, prominent x and y descents, a pericardial knock, and pericardial calcification is constrictive pericarditis.

## Q11. Tricuspid Regurgitation
**Question ID:** Q6193226
**Question:** A patient with right heart failure has giant v waves in the jugular venous pulse, a pulsatile liver, and a pansystolic murmur at the left sternal border that increases on inspiration. What is the lesion?
- A) Tricuspid regurgitation
- B) Tricuspid stenosis
- C) Mitral regurgitation
- D) Pulmonary stenosis

**Answer:** A) Tricuspid regurgitation

**Explanation:**
Giant *v* waves with a prominent *y* descent and absent *x* descent, a pulsatile (systolic) liver, and a pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge that increases on inspiration (**Carvallo sign**) define **Tricuspid Regurgitation**. The inspiratory increase confirms right-sided origin.

**Take Home Message:** Giant v waves, a pulsatile liver, and a pansystolic murmur louder on inspiration mean tricuspid regurgitation; tricuspid stenosis gives a large a wave and a diastolic murmur.

## Q12. Enterococcal Endocarditis Treatment
**Question ID:** Q4952304
**Question:** A patient with a penicillin allergy has enterococcal infective endocarditis. Which antibiotic regimen is the best choice?
- A) Penicillin G with gentamicin
- B) Ampicillin with gentamicin
- C) Ceftriaxone alone
- D) Vancomycin with gentamicin

**Answer:** D) Vancomycin with gentamicin

**Explanation:**
Enterococcal endocarditis requires a cell-wall-active agent (ampicillin/penicillin G) PLUS **gentamicin** for bactericidal synergy. In penicillin-allergic or penicillin-resistant cases, **Vancomycin + Gentamicin** is used. Cephalosporins (ceftriaxone) are naturally inactive against enterococci.

**Take Home Message:** Enterococcal endocarditis needs a cell-wall agent plus gentamicin; with penicillin allergy or resistance, use vancomycin, and never rely on a cephalosporin.

## Q13. Blood Culture Collection Protocol for Endocarditis
**Question ID:** Q4914422
**Question:** A patient with suspected infective endocarditis has not yet had antibiotics. How should the blood cultures be collected?
- A) At least two to three separate sets, aerobic and anaerobic, from different sites spaced over time, before antibiotics
- B) Three sets drawn within five minutes from the same venepuncture site
- C) A single set of one aerobic and one anaerobic bottle
- D) Cultures only after empirical antibiotics have been started

**Answer:** A) At least two to three separate sets, aerobic and anaerobic, from different sites spaced over time, before antibiotics

**Explanation:**
Endocarditis causes continuous bacteraemia. Standard protocol: draw **2 to 3 separate sets** (aerobic + anaerobic bottles per set) from different venipuncture sites spaced 30 to 60 minutes apart within 24 hours, BEFORE initiating antibiotics.

**Take Home Message:** Take several separate aerobic-and-anaerobic blood culture sets from different sites over time, before antibiotics; persistent bacteraemia is a major Duke criterion.

## Q15. Anticoagulation in Valvular Atrial Fibrillation
**Question ID:** Q5632039
**Question:** A 24-year-old woman with rheumatic mitral stenosis develops atrial fibrillation. Which anticoagulant is appropriate?
- A) Warfarin
- B) Dabigatran
- C) Apixaban
- D) Aspirin alone

**Answer:** A) Warfarin

**Explanation:**
Atrial fibrillation in moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis or with mechanical prosthetic heart valves is classified as **valvular AF**. Full oral anticoagulation with **Warfarin** is mandatory. Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs like dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban) are **CONTRAINDICATED**.

**Take Home Message:** Atrial fibrillation with rheumatic mitral stenosis is valvular and anticoagulated with warfarin; direct oral anticoagulants are contraindicated.

## Q16. Infective Endocarditis Risk Stratification (Low-Risk vs High-Risk Lesions)
**Question ID:** Q3586010
**Question:** When deciding which cardiac lesions carry a risk of infective endocarditis, which of the following is the LEAST likely to predispose to it?
- A) A small secundum atrial septal defect
- B) A bicuspid aortic valve
- C) A ventricular septal defect
- D) A prosthetic valve

**Answer:** A) A small secundum atrial septal defect

**Explanation:**
A small ostium secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) carries the lowest endocarditis risk because it is a low-pressure, low-velocity shunt without high-velocity jet turbulent damage to the endocardium. 
*High-risk / predisposing lesions:* Prosthetic valves, prior endocarditis, ventricular septal defect (VSD), bicuspid aortic valve, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HOCM), cardiac implantable devices, and severe valvular lesions.

**Take Home Message:** A small secundum atrial septal defect rarely causes endocarditis; prosthetic valves, prior endocarditis, ventricular septal defects, and bicuspid valves are high-risk.

## Q17. Antibiotic Prophylaxis Indications for Infective Endocarditis
**Question ID:** Q7794661
**Question:** All of the following are correct regarding antibiotic prophylaxis for infective endocarditis EXCEPT:
- A) It is indicated for patients with a prosthetic valve or prosthetic valve material.
- B) It is indicated after a previous episode of infective endocarditis.
- C) It is recommended for every patient with a small atrial septal defect before dental work.

**Answer:** C) It is recommended for every patient with a small atrial septal defect before dental work.

**Explanation:**
Endocarditis prophylaxis before dental procedures (manipulating gingival tissue / periapical region) is reserved ONLY for the highest-risk cardiac conditions:
1. **Prosthetic heart valves** or prosthetic material used for valve repair.
2. **Previous episode of infective endocarditis**.
3. **Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD)** or repaired CHD with residual defects / within 6 months of repair with prosthetic material.
4. **Cardiac transplant recipients** with valvulopathy.
*Standard Regimen:* **Amoxicillin 2 g PO** (or Ampicillin 2 g IV/IM) 30 to 60 mins before procedure. (If penicillin-allergic: Azithromycin 500 mg, Doxycycline 100 mg, or Cephalexin 2 g).

**Take Home Message:** Endocarditis prophylaxis with amoxicillin before dental work is reserved for the highest-risk hearts (prosthetic valves, prior endocarditis, certain congenital disease), not a small atrial septal defect.

## Q18. Bedside Murmur Maneuvers
**Question ID:** Q4742106
**Question:** Identify the correct statements concerning the bedside maneuvers used to characterise murmurs:
1. Right-sided murmurs increase on inspiration (Carvallo sign).
2. The aortic regurgitation murmur is heard best with the patient leaning forward in held expiration.
3. Left-sided murmurs are loudest during inspiration.
4. Sustained handgrip softens the murmur of mitral regurgitation.

- A) 1, 2 and 3 only
- B) 1 and 2 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only

**Answer:** B) 1 and 2 only

**Explanation:**
- *Inspiration:* Increases venous return to right heart -> **Right-sided murmurs increase** (**Carvallo sign**).
- *Expiration:* Increases venous return to left heart -> **Left-sided murmurs increase** (Statement 3 is False).
- *Aortic Regurgitation:* Early diastolic murmur is heard best with patient **sitting forward in held expiration** at lower left sternal border.
- *Handgrip (Increased Afterload):* **Intensifies** regurgitant murmurs (MR, AR, VSD) and **softens** Aortic Stenosis & HOCM (Statement 4 is False).

**Take Home Message:** Right-sided murmurs are loudest on inspiration and left-sided on expiration; handgrip (more afterload) intensifies regurgitant murmurs and softens aortic stenosis.

## Q19. Myocarditis Etiology & Pathogenesis
**Question ID:** Q6466740
**Question:** Which of the following statements about myocarditis are true?
1. It can present subclinically with palpitations and a raised troponin.
2. Myocarditis never progresses to dilated cardiomyopathy.
3. Diphtheritic myocarditis results from bacteraemic spread of the organism.
4. Viral infection is the commonest cause in developed countries.

- A) 1, 2 and 4 only
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1 and 4 only

**Answer:** C) 1 and 4 only

**Explanation:**
- *Statement 1 is True:* Myocarditis ranges from subclinical illness (palpitations, raised troponin, mild ECG changes) to fulminant heart failure / sudden cardiac death.
- *Statement 2 is False:* Myocarditis CAN progress to **Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM)**.
- *Statement 3 is False:* Diphtheritic myocarditis is caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae **exotoxin**, NOT bacteraemic invasion.
- *Statement 4 is True:* Viral infection (Coxsackie B, Parvovirus B19, HHV-6, Adenovirus) is the most common cause in developed countries.

**Take Home Message:** Most myocarditis is viral; diphtheritic myocarditis is toxin-mediated, and any myocarditis can progress to a dilated cardiomyopathy.

## Q20. Graham-Steell Murmur
**Question ID:** Q5631662
**Question:** A patient with severe mitral stenosis and pulmonary hypertension develops a high-pitched early diastolic murmur at the upper left sternal border that increases on inspiration; the pulse pressure is normal. Which lesion is responsible?
- A) Pulmonary regurgitation
- B) Aortic regurgitation
- C) Tricuspid stenosis
- D) Mitral regurgitation

**Answer:** A) Pulmonary regurgitation

**Explanation:**
The **Graham-Steell murmur** is a high-pitched early diastolic decrescendo murmur heard at the upper left sternal border. It represents **functional pulmonary regurgitation** caused by severe pulmonary hypertension (classically secondary to severe mitral stenosis). 
Distinction from Aortic Regurgitation: Graham-Steell murmur increases on inspiration (right-sided) and does NOT produce a wide pulse pressure or collapsing pulse.

**Take Home Message:** The Graham-Steell murmur is the early diastolic murmur of pulmonary regurgitation from pulmonary hypertension, mimicking aortic regurgitation but without a collapsing pulse.

## Q23. Non-Infective (Nonbacterial) Endocarditis (Marantic & Libman-Sacks)
**Question ID:** Q9642199
**Question:** All of the following are correct regarding non-infective (nonbacterial) endocarditis EXCEPT:
- A) Marantic (nonbacterial thrombotic) endocarditis is associated with advanced malignancy and chronic wasting illness.
- B) Libman-Sacks endocarditis occurs in systemic lupus erythematosus and the antiphospholipid syndrome.
- C) Libman-Sacks vegetations are typically sterile and can be on either surface of the valve.
- D) These vegetations contain abundant bacteria and require prolonged antibiotics.

**Answer:** D) These vegetations contain abundant bacteria and require prolonged antibiotics.

**Explanation:**
Nonbacterial vegetations are **STERILE** (culture-negative, no bacteria, no antibiotics indicated).
- **Marantic Endocarditis (NBTE):** Sterile fibrin-platelet vegetations on normal valves in advanced cancers (mucin-secreting adenocarcinomas) and chronic wasting diseases. High embolic risk.
- **Libman-Sacks Endocarditis:** Sterile verrucous lesions occurring in **SLE** and **Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)**; can occur on BOTH sides of the valve leaflets (usually mitral).

**Take Home Message:** Marantic (cancer) and Libman-Sacks (lupus and antiphospholipid syndrome) endocarditis are sterile; treat the underlying disease and anticoagulate, not with antibiotics.

## Q24. Second Heart Sound (S2) Splitting Patterns
**Question ID:** Q1458429
**Question:** Which of the following statements about the second heart sound are correct?
1. Fixed splitting is characteristic of an atrial septal defect.
2. A loud pulmonary component indicates pulmonary hypertension.
3. Wide splitting occurs in right bundle branch block.
4. A loud aortic component occurs in systemic hypertension.

- A) 1, 2 and 3 only
- B) 1 and 2 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only
- D) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Answer:** D) 1, 2, 3 and 4

**Explanation:**
All four statements are correct:
1. **Fixed Split S2:** Characteristic of **Atrial Septal Defect (ASD)** (unaffected by respiration).
2. **Wide Split S2:** Caused by delayed P2 (e.g., **RBBB**, Pulmonary Stenosis).
3. **Paradoxical / Reversed Split S2:** P2 closes before A2 (splits on expiration) due to delayed A2 (e.g., **LBBB**, severe Aortic Stenosis).
4. **Loud A2:** **Systemic Hypertension** (soft A2 in severe calcific AS).
5. **Loud P2:** **Pulmonary Hypertension** (soft P2 in severe PS).

**Take Home Message:** Fixed split means atrial septal defect, wide split right bundle branch block, loud aortic component occurs in systemic hypertension and a loud pulmonary component means pulmonary hypertension.

## Q25. Mitral Regurgitation Signs & Causes
**Question ID:** Q5001748
**Question:** A patient has a high-pitched pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla, with a soft first heart sound and a third heart sound. Which lesion is this?
- A) Mitral regurgitation
- B) Mitral stenosis
- C) Aortic stenosis
- D) Tricuspid regurgitation

**Answer:** A) Mitral regurgitation

Mitral Regurgitation gives a blowing **pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla**, a **soft S1** (incomplete valve closure), forceful/displaced apex, and often an **S3**.

**Take Home Message:** A pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla with a soft first sound and a third heart sound is mitral regurgitation.

## Q27. Tricuspid Stenosis
**Question ID:** Q7966937
**Question:** A patient with rheumatic heart disease has a large a wave in the jugular venous pulse, a blunted y descent, and a mid-diastolic murmur at the lower left sternal border that increases on inspiration. Which valve lesion is this?
- A) Mitral stenosis
- B) Tricuspid stenosis
- C) Tricuspid regurgitation

A **large *a* wave** (vigorous RA contraction against resistance), **blunted *y* descent** (slow RV filling), and **mid-diastolic murmur at lower left sternal border increasing on inspiration** define **Tricuspid Stenosis**. Almost always rheumatic in origin and coexists with mitral stenosis.

**Take Home Message:** Tricuspid stenosis gives a large a wave, a blunted y descent, and an inspiration-augmented mid-diastolic murmur, usually rheumatic and alongside mitral stenosis.

## Q28. Chronic Aortic Regurgitation Signs (Wide Pulse Pressure)

**Question:** Which of the following statements about the signs of chronic aortic regurgitation are correct?
1. A collapsing (water-hammer) pulse with a wide pulse pressure is characteristic.
2. The Austin-Flint murmur is a mid-diastolic apical rumble of severe regurgitation.
3. Pulsus parvus et tardus is the typical pulsus.
4. Traube sign is a pistol-shot sound over the femoral arteries.

- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 1, 2 and 3 only
- C) 1, 2 and 4 only

**Answer:** C) 1, 2 and 4 only

**Explanation:**
Chronic aortic regurgitation causes a large stroke volume ejected into a low-resistance vascular bed, resulting in a **wide pulse pressure** and numerous famous peripheral signs:
1. **Corrigan / Water-Hammer Pulse:** Rapidly swelling and collapsing arterial pulse (Statement 1 True).
2. **Austin-Flint Murmur:** Soft, low-pitched mid-diastolic apical rumble of severe AR (Statement 2 True).
3. **Traube Sign:** Pistol-shot booming sound heard over the femoral arteries (Statement 4 True).
4. **Duroziez Sign:** To-and-fro murmur heard over the femoral artery upon compression.
5. **Quincke Sign:** Capillary pulsations visible in the nailbeds.
6. **de Musset Sign:** Head-bobbing synchronous with the pulse.
7. **Hill Sign:** Popliteal SBP exceeding brachial SBP by > 20 mmHg (most sensitive physical sign for AR).

*Statement 3 is False:* **Pulsus parvus et tardus** (small, slow-rising pulse) is the hallmark of **Aortic Stenosis**, NOT Aortic Regurgitation.

**Take Home Message:** Aortic regurgitation gives a collapsing pulse and wide-pulse-pressure signs (Corrigan, de Musset, Quincke nail-bed pulsation, Traube, Duroziez); parvus et tardus is aortic stenosis.

## Q29. Rheumatic Fever Secondary Prophylaxis Duration Guidelines
**Question ID:** Q9890036
**Question:** A child with rheumatic carditis and residual valvular heart disease needs secondary prophylaxis. For how long should benzathine penicillin be continued?
- A) For 5 years or until age 21
- B) For 10 years or until age 40, whichever is longer (lifelong if severe)
- C) For 1 year only
- D) No prophylaxis is needed once the acute episode resolves

**Answer:** B) For 10 years or until age 40, whichever is longer (lifelong if severe)

**Explanation:**
Secondary Prophylaxis Regimens for Rheumatic Fever (Benzathine Penicillin G 1.2 MU IM q3-4 weeks):
1. **Rheumatic Fever WITHOUT Carditis:** 5 years or until age 21, whichever is longer.
2. **Rheumatic Fever WITH Carditis, NO residual valvular disease:** 10 years or until age 21, whichever is longer.
3. **Rheumatic Fever WITH Carditis AND residual valvular disease:** 10 years or until age 40, whichever is longer (often lifelong if severe disease).

**Take Home Message:** Rheumatic carditis with residual valve disease needs benzathine penicillin until age 40 or for 10 years, lifelong if severe.

## Q30. Differentiating Constrictive Pericarditis vs Restrictive Cardiomyopathy
**Question ID:** Q9290908
**Question:** All of the following favour constrictive pericarditis over restrictive cardiomyopathy EXCEPT:
- A) Pericardial calcification or thickening on imaging
- B) A pericardial knock
- C) Respiratory variation in ventricular filling (ventricular interdependence) on echocardiography
- D) Marked biatrial enlargement with very high natriuretic peptide levels

**Answer:** D) Marked biatrial enlargement with very high natriuretic peptide levels

**Explanation:**
- **Favors Constrictive Pericarditis:** Pericardial calcification/thickening on CT/MRI, **Pericardial knock**, exaggerated **respiratory variation in ventricular filling** (ventricular interdependence / septal bounce), dip-and-plateau (square-root) pressure tracings. Curable via **pericardiectomy**.
- **Favors Restrictive Cardiomyopathy (e.g., Amyloidosis):** **Marked biatrial enlargement**, thick ventricular myocardium, **very high BNP / NT-proBNP levels**, and high RV systolic pressure (>50 mmHg).

**Take Home Message:** Pericardial calcification, a knock, and respiratory ventricular interdependence favour constrictive pericarditis; big atria with very high natriuretic peptides favour restrictive cardiomyopathy.

## Q31. Combined Rheumatic Valve Lesions (AR + MR)
**Question ID:** Q8546176
**Question:** A woman with rheumatic heart disease has both an early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge and a pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla. Which combination of lesions does she have?
- A) Mitral stenosis and tricuspid regurgitation
- B) Aortic stenosis and mitral stenosis
- C) Aortic regurgitation and mitral regurgitation
- D) Aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis

**Answer:** C) Aortic regurgitation and mitral regurgitation

**Explanation:**
- Early diastolic murmur at left sternal border = **Aortic Regurgitation**.
- Pansystolic murmur at apex radiating to axilla = **Mitral Regurgitation**.
Rheumatic heart disease frequently produces mixed and multi-valve lesions (mitral + aortic valve involvement).

**Take Home Message:** Rheumatic disease often gives mixed lesions; an early diastolic murmur plus an apical pansystolic murmur to the axilla is combined aortic and mitral regurgitation.
