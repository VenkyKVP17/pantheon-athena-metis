# Neurology QBank

## Q1. Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis of Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)
**Question ID:** Q5775101
**Question:** A 20 year old male presents with sudden onset severe headache and vomiting. On examination, he has neck stiffness. A CT scan of the brain shows hydrocephalus. What is the most likely diagnosis?
- A) Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus
- B) Bacterial Meningitis
- C) Viral Encephalitis
- D) Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

**Answer:** D) Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

**Explanation:**
- The most likely diagnosis is **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)**.
- **Clinical Presentation:** SAH classically presents with sudden onset, severe headache ("thunderclap headache" - worst headache of life), accompanied by vomiting and neck stiffness (meningismus).
- **Imaging & Complications:** Non-contrast CT (NCCT) of the brain is the initial diagnostic test of choice. Acute hydrocephalus can develop secondary to blood obstructing CSF outflow at arachnoid villi or cerebral aqueduct.
- **Diagnostic Sensitivity:** In ~10% of SAH cases, initial NCCT can be normal. If clinical suspicion remains high despite negative CT, a lumbar puncture (LP) is indicated to detect xanthochromia.

**Take Home Message:** Sudden onset severe "thunderclap" headache with neck stiffness and hydrocephalus is classic for Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH). Initial NCCT is normal in ~10% of cases, requiring LP for xanthochromia.
